PART II. DETAILED MAP OF MODEL OF THE PHENOMENON

A detailed reference guide for reading, applying, and criticizing Model of the Phenomenon by Roman Dudnikov. It distinguishes the original work, explanatory notes, editorial interpretations, and independent criticism.

Governing principle. An exposition of Model of the Phenomenon should not silently introduce a subject, an external world, time, space, cause, process, or independent entities as the model's grounds. Clarifying terms require the same standards of justification as the concepts under discussion. The model's own vocabulary and the vocabulary of external inquiry must not be conflated without explanation.

0. Purpose and limits of this part

0.1. This part is intended for readers who study, explain, criticize, translate, formalize, or compare Model of the Phenomenon with other philosophical positions.

0.2. An accurate exposition conveys the position as formulated instead of silently replacing it with familiar accounts of consciousness, the subject, or metaphysical monism. Substantive accuracy matters more than conformity to recognizable academic categories, stylistic polish, or a hypothetical reviewer's expectations.

0.3. Four types of material should be distinguished: the text of (A); its definitions and examples; editorial or research reconstructions; and independent criticism. Any consequential disputed claim should be identified accordingly.

0.4. New conclusions do not automatically become part of the starting theses or direct quotations. Where the guide differs from a source or explanatory note, the exact location and character of the difference should be stated rather than silently replacing the source.

0.5. The map does not certify the truth of the first thesis for every reader, remove the need for intellectual criticism, or declare every possible philosophical problem false. It provides a framework for dealing accurately with the chosen position.

1. Provenance and evidential status

[T] Unalterable theses: the five sentences in Section 2. They are preserved verbatim, in the original order, without supposedly improving their wording.

[S] Original definitions from the “Terms” section of (A). They explain the terms as used in the work, but are formally distinct from the five theses.

[E] Interpretations and examples in (A): its seven thematic areas, the one-line diagram, light, noise, film, dreaming, doubt, and other universes. They illustrate modes of inquiry, not separately proved universal theorems.

[AN] Explanatory notes recorded in the map: “one” is itself a concept; time, space, subject, cause, process, and necessity are not introduced as independent grounds, nor are their negations turned into properties. Distinction has no separate agent; green is distinguished by its greenness; the world and the content of experience remain; the model applies to itself; inquiry need not have an aim or dissolve a problem.

[R] Research reconstruction: an analytical proposal, scheme, interpretation, or logical check. Its force depends on arguments and its relation to the sources, not the identity of its proponent.

[X] External formulation: terms, questions, and methods drawn from metaphysics, philosophy of mind, logic, or the natural sciences. Such approaches may be used in external inquiry, but they are not thereby required components of the model.

[?] Open question: a passage for which the source does not fix one unequivocal meaning or where formulations remain in tension. No gap may be filled by fabricating a claim attributed to the original work.

Texts and interpretations. The five theses are quoted unchanged. Explanations help establish their meaning and scope without replacing the original sentences. In a disputed case, the formulations can be compared to determine whether the difference is verbal, substantive, editorial, or logical.

2. The unalterable core: five theses [T]

  1. Everything is one phenomenon.
  2. The phenomenon is sensory experiencing.
  3. A distinction within sensory experiencing is a concept.
  4. Concepts structure the phenomenon into experience.
  5. Experience is the phenomenon structured through concepts.

Interpretive restrictions. “Is” must not be replaced with “produces,” “generates,” “consists of,” or “causes”; “one” with “one subject,” “one thing,” “universal consciousness,” or “a single global perspective”; “concept” with “an observer's thought,” “word,” or “mental act”; or “experience” with the end product of a process.

The five theses constitute the entire model in the sense of its original formulation. Subsequent definitions, examples, and inquiries explore applications. They do not constitute a hidden sixth, seventh, or further thesis.

3. Foundational principles of interpretation

3.1. “One” is not an entity, number, or property [T]+[S]+[AN]

“One” is also a concept, a distinction of the one phenomenon within experience. It does not designate a special substance, primordial matter, world-object, absolute subject, universal container, negative “non-nature,” or positive “nature” to be given properties. Its function is, above all, not to introduce independent further grounds or take distinctions as independently existing ontological divisions.

Inaccurate: “There are initially separate objects, which are subsequently united in one experiencing”; “the phenomenon is the most fundamental thing and has properties X, Y, and Z”; “the phenomenon has a single perspective.”
More accurate: “‘One’ does not denote a numerically unique object; it provides no ground for treating what is distinguished as independent ontological grounds.” But neither should “the phenomenon is devoid of multiplicity” be derived as an additional metaphysical predicate.

Refusing independent grounds must not introduce a new positive ground under the name “phenomenon.” Neither positive nor negative properties should be ascribed to the phenomenon itself. “Has no time” is not a property called “timelessness,” but a refusal to treat the concept of time as its necessary ground. A risk within the model is reifying the concept of the phenomenon itself. The rigorous formulation limits explanatory commitments; it does not define a “real nature” through negation.

3.2. “Phenomenon” is not a model of the world [T]+[S]+[AN]

Model of the Phenomenon is not cosmology, physics, an account of the constitution of matter, or a description of the only possible world. A world or worlds, dream, film, hallucination, life, death, causality, physical laws, and their absence in dreams can all be distinguished as ways of structuring experience within the phenomenon.

This does not mean that the entire world is a dream, that the physical world is absent, or that scientific regularities have been refuted. Describing a particular world through its concepts differs from making those concepts necessary properties of the phenomenon itself. The latter is not adopted within the position.

3.3. Experiencing requires no separate experiencer [T]+[S]+[E]+[AN]

“Sensory experiencing” has a deliberately broad meaning: sensations, emotions, thoughts, and experienced states (A, “Terms”). It is not confined to inputs from particular sense organs. “There is experiencing” does not require a separate owner. “Mine,” “his,” “hers,” and “someone else's” are distinctions within experience, not original bearers of it.

Inaccurate as a necessary starting condition: “A subject distinguishes the content of experiencing”; “experience appears in an observer”; “the phenomenon appears to someone.”
More accurate for philosophical analysis: “Experiencing is distinguished as ‘I see,’ ‘she feels,’ or ‘someone observes’; an observer is a concept within that experience.” Ordinary expressions themselves remain acceptable.

The absence of a mandatory owner does not deny pain, other people, their social importance, or scientific inquiry into consciousness.

3.4. Distinction is not an action [T]+[S]+[AN]

There is no separate act, agent, mechanism, power, or concept acting on experiencing to add qualities to it. Green is distinguished by its greenness, in virtue of being green. A concept names the distinction in the model's language, not a controlling thing.

Inaccurate: “The concept of green colors a previously colorless experiencing”; “a distinguishing consciousness creates a boundary between colors.”
More accurate: “Green is distinguished by its greenness; ‘greenness’ is neither a second entity nor a cause of green.”

The verb “structure” in the fourth thesis is preserved exactly. It must not be read as the causal production of experience from preexisting unformed matter.

3.5. Distinction does not entail an original separation [T]+[S]+[AN]

Naming two distinctions does not establish two independent substances. “I” and “world,” “real” and “illusory,” “here” and “there,” or “cause” and “effect” can be substantive distinctions in experience without becoming ontological first principles. “No reason to introduce an independent entity” does not mean “the experiencing, object, or practical relation has been erased.”

3.6. A concept is a system of distinctions, not a verbal label [S]

The published English definition in (A) reads: “Concept — a system of distinctions. Each distinction separates one thing from another, establishing a direction ‘from’ and ‘toward.’ The distinction itself, in turn, exists as a system of distinctions.” This systematicity concerns mutual conceptual determination, without a directing subject, required first element, or temporal unfolding. [AN; see I.12.3] It should not be represented as independent branches, isolated clusters of atoms, or a one-way chain in which one distinction determines the next. Distinctions may determine one another, even where particular experience names them “past” and “future.” This does not entail a universal law of mutual causal influence among physical objects or a new property of the phenomenon.

Formal trees, graphs, sets, nested intervals, or recursive functions are external representations; they are neither identical to the phenomenon nor its spatial or temporal anatomy. Infinite mathematical nesting is an additional requirement imposed by a particular reconstruction, not a discovered property of the phenomenon.

3.7. Experience is not a produced result [T]+[S]+[E]

Theses 4 and 5 describe experience with different emphases. There is no stage “before concepts,” “after concepts,” independent raw material, or operation that processes it. (A) explicitly states: “Experience is not the result of a process” and rejects “pure experiencing” prior to distinction. Images of undifferentiated noise are illustrative schemes, not claims about an actual preconceptual era of the phenomenon.

3.8. Time, space, subject, cause, and necessity are not grounds of the phenomenon [E]+[AN]

This is not a trivial reservation that “the model has not yet discussed time.” It is a substantive refusal, within the adopted model, to place time, space, subject, causality, modality, beginning, end, or process behind or above conceptual distinctions as an independent existing ground of the phenomenon.

Within experience there may be distinctions of duration, spatial distance, cause, effect, choice, mortality, pain, error, science, and justice. They are not abolished. Nor are they transferred to the phenomenon as necessary properties beyond concepts.

The refusal must not be weakened automatically to “perhaps an independent fundamental time or subject exists, but the model has yet to speak about it.” Within the adopted position, such a ground is not accepted. Whether the refusal is justified may still be examined in external criticism, labeled [X].

Nor should the refusal be strengthened automatically into external factual claims that “physical time does not exist,” “biological death is unreal,” or “science is false.” These are different questions and do not follow from a change of terminology.

3.9. The phenomenon has no properties outside distinctions [E]+[AN]

“Green,” “hard,” “objective,” “infinite,” “causal,” and “changing” are distinctions, not supra-experiential properties of a special object called “the phenomenon.” Even “the phenomenon has no properties” is a conceptual formulation; it does not introduce “propertylessness” as the metaphysical characteristic of a substance. A useful check is whether “one” has silently acquired a positive property just where the need to attribute properties was being rejected.

3.10. The model neither creates, devalues, nor improves experience [E]+[AN]

The model is itself a concept through which experience may be structured differently, but not necessarily “better,” “purer,” “truer,” or “closer to reality.” The world remains the world; pain remains pain; conversation remains conversation; scientific findings remain findings within the relevant experience. The model promises neither an end to suffering, an abolition of responsibility, nor superiority over other descriptions. Inquiry concerns how a description takes shape and which starting assumptions it no longer requires.

3.11. Self-application without exceptions [T]+[S]+[E]+[AN]

The five theses, “one,” the model, writer and reader, an article, criticism, artificial intelligence, an algorithm, and doubt are all included in the same examination. None becomes an external observer of the model or its ground. This does not confer infallibility: logical error remains error under the applicable criteria of assessment.

3.12. Independence of inquiry without defensive overreach [AN]

Inquiry does not require prior endorsement by an established tradition or the dilution of the starting position for reputational reasons. Conversely, rhetorical defensiveness, unsupported universal victories, pretended deductions, and replacing arguments with authority do not establish the truth of any claim. Criticism calls for a substantive response: “that too is a concept” is not one by itself.

3.13. The copula “is” and no separate nature behind experiencing [AN; see I.10.1]

The second thesis does not treat “sensory experiencing” as a manner in which a separate phenomenon manifests itself. No independently existing thing called “the phenomenon” is placed behind experiencing. Terms in the formulation are themselves distinctions within experience. Expressions such as “the phenomenon possesses experiencing,” “the phenomenon takes the form of experiencing,” or “pure phenomenon becomes experience” silently introduce additional relations or processes. More precise philosophical wording would be: “‘phenomenon’ and ‘sensory experiencing’ distinguish in words what those words do not divide into two entities.” Removing a verbal division does not imply the literal erasure of qualitative content or a temporal removal of words. English is is used in the published thesis and should not be replaced with a productive or possessive verb merely to make the sentence sound more familiar.

3.14. Invariance without an additional property of changelessness [AN; R]

Explanations of the model include the formulation “the phenomenon is unchanging,” but it does not establish changelessness as a property of a hidden thing. Change, becoming, and causal histories are distinguished within experience; the model does not require an objective process of change in the phenomenon itself. An “eternal, motionless substrate” would be another positive ground; “perhaps the phenomenon changes, but we do not know yet” would be a different research position.

4. The self-applying composition of the five theses

The model is also presented in a single line in (A):

… Phenomenon = Experiencing ⇄ Distinction ⇄ Concept ⇄ Experience = Phenomenon …

This is a conceptual aid to reading, not a quantitative equation or causal chain.

  • The first thesis distinguishes everything as one phenomenon.
  • The second adds no second substance: the phenomenon is sensory experiencing.
  • The third adds no separate distinguisher: a distinction within sensory experiencing is a concept.
  • The fourth posits no action of a concept on raw material: concepts structure the phenomenon into experience.
  • The fifth returns “experience” to “phenomenon” without producing a new object: experience is the phenomenon structured through concepts.

Self-reference of the formulation [AN]. The model, its meaning, rereading with memory of the model, and a different conceptual organization of reading are again experience within the phenomenon. The last thesis returns semantically to the first. This does not prove, in formal logic, that thesis 1 follows from thesis 5, nor does it posit a real chronological loop. “Memory” here means the experienced concept of memory, not a required property of the phenomenon named “the past.”

Diagramming rule. A one-way production chain, “phenomenon → experiencing → processing → experience,” does not faithfully represent the five theses. A two-way, self-applying depiction accompanied by “distinguished in the text, not divided in the phenomenon” better conveys the intended relationship.

Question for each term: does it name a distinction within experience, or has an independent entity been introduced without notice? In the latter case, either the wording needs clarification or the status of the external assumption should be marked [X]/[?].

6. Ordinary language is not forbidden; ontological assumptions require scrutiny

English sentences such as “I see,” “yesterday,” “the chair is real,” and “the cause of the breakdown” are perfectly ordinary expressions. They need not be mechanically rewritten into the model's specialized vocabulary. What matters is what a particular assertion actually claims, not whether it uses pronouns, verbs, predicates, or tense. When speaking of the phenomenon itself, grammatical forms should not silently import a separate actor or independent ground.

Formulation requiring attention Potential shift in meaning More appropriate wording for the relevant philosophical analysis
“The subject distinguishes.” Introduces an agent “The ‘I’ is distinguished within experiencing.”
“The concept produces green.” Implies causal production “Green is distinguished by its greenness.”
“First the phenomenon, then distinction.” Implies sequential stages “Phenomenon, distinction, and experience are distinguished in the model's formulation.”
“The phenomenon appears at a moment.” Introduces time and cause “The phenomenon is; a moment is distinguished within experience.”
“Pure experiencing existed before all experience.” Implies a preconceptual history “The basic terms do not refer to successive stages.”
“Concepts form boundaries in reality.” Implies an action upon substance “Boundaries exist as distinctions within experience.”
“One contains elements as a vessel does.” Adds a container and contents “What is distinguished need not be independently grounded.”
“The phenomenon possesses eternity.” Makes the concept of time a property “Eternity is a concept in experience; the phenomenon is not described in terms of duration.”
“Physics is wrong.” Confuses a world with the phenomenon “Physical laws belong to conceptually structured experience of a world.”
“The problem of consciousness does not exist.” Premature victory claim “The starting distinction that makes this formulation seem compulsory can be examined.”
“The model proves everything.” Shields the model from criticism “The model offers an approach to examination; any conclusion must be argued separately.”
“Our method purifies experience.” Implies an agent, value, and required outcome “Experience may be distinguished differently when considered through the model; the model does not act on it.”

Ordinary organizing expressions—“level,” “sequence,” “analysis,” “let us turn to,” “first,” “then,” “within,” “structure,” and “system”—can describe the organization of an explanation. This organization should not be projected onto the phenomenon. When a philosophical passage is ambiguous, the distinction can be made explicit: “the order of exposition, not a sequence of existence.”

The coin analogy [AN]. Both attributing time to the phenomenon and attributing the “absence of time” may assume that time is a characteristic that must determine the phenomenon. The model does not have to select one side of that question. This does not turn talk about experienced time into a claim that clocks, memories, death, and sequences are absent from experience.

“Before,” “after,” and “appeared” in critical discussion [R]. Such expressions are legitimate for organizing a narrative, but do not thereby refer to processes in the phenomenon or the chronological origin of a concept. Asking “Where does this distinction acquire its characteristics?” can mean examining conceptual determinacy rather than locating an event in which a substance acquired a property.

7. A possible procedure for examining a particular case (neither a law nor a required outcome)

The following is one way of organizing research, not a process occurring within the phenomenon.

Step A. Preserve the initial statement rather than correcting it in advance: for example, “I see green,” “Anna is in pain,” “time passes,” or “nothing exists after death.”

Step B. Identify distinctions within the statement: “I,” “see,” “green,” “Anna,” “pain,” “after,” and “nothing.” This requires no independent agent of distinction.

Step C. Examine systems of distinctions: how each expression is determined by other distinctions, experiencings, and comparisons. Concepts are not treated as independently acting parts. [AN] The five theses are considered together; no isolated atom, first cause, originating moment, or unique mathematical graph need be found.

Step D. Ask whether an independent ground has been introduced. Has a name for a distinction been made into a claim that “there must therefore be a subject, time, space, object, or cause underlying everything”? An additional premise is identified only if it is actually present in the argument being examined.

Step E. If an additional ground is found, assess its role. What in this account requires introducing it? If nothing does, its necessity has not been established. Any substantive question that remains open after this analysis is still a question; it does not automatically invalidate the model.

Step F. Preserve experienced differences without introducing a “property of changelessness.” Conceptual consideration does not make pain cease to hurt, the world disappear, people unreal, an instrument stop registering color, or responsibility vanish. [AN] Just as distinguishing brushstrokes does not erase the picture, examining something through the model does not create an operative force that transforms experience. The phenomenon itself does not become a substance with a newly attributed property of immutability.

Step G. Apply the same scrutiny to the analysis itself: its vocabulary, premises, and inferences, regardless of who conducts the inquiry.

Possible presentation: original assertion → distinctions → their mutual determination → additional ground discovered (if any) → content of the examination → open questions (if any). “No additional ground identified” is also an acceptable outcome, neither a failure of the method nor a victory for the model. Arrows show the order of presentation, not a process in the phenomenon. The outline is optional and belongs to work on the text, not to the model as such.

8. Examples: original and independent analyses

8.1. Green: distinction rather than an action by a concept [S]+[E]+[AN]

Original meaning: there is experiencing of green; green is distinguished by its greenness. “Greenness” is neither a property of some separate colorless substrate nor the cause of green's arising. No agent selects the boundary of green. Describing its color remains legitimate.

Common substitution: “First a subject receives an undifferentiated signal, then the concept of green extracts the color.”
Accurate formulation: “Green is distinguished as green; calling it ‘green’ adds no second entity to experiencing.”

8.2. White light [E]

Light, colors, shapes, and images are not four independent substances, nor does the analogy put forward a physical theory of the light spectrum. It illustrates how one experiencing can be described through distinctions of color, shape, and image, without causally adding new entities. It does not require the historical existence of a stage of pure white light before distinction.

8.3. Noise [E]

Red and green, yellow and blue dots; a pattern, a field, stripes; structures and systems. Differences are distinguished through the actual differences among their combinations, not because a law or subject imposes order from outside. Noise, structure, and systems are not posited as successive stages of production. A description that does not rely on familiar time and space expresses the absence of their necessary ontological priority, not a physical theory of an image.

8.4. The “I,” kettle, and philosopher [E]

These simple and sometimes self-ironic examples show that examining something through the model does not depend on the investigator's standing as a philosopher or the complexity of the subject. There is no requirement to replace them with “global consciousness,” “quantum mechanics,” or “intersubjective synthesis.”

  • “I”: experiencing “I am,” distinctions of “I/not-I,” and systems including “mine,” “with me,” “this/that/there.” No hidden owner need be discovered.
  • “Kettle”: an ordinary thing distinguished through shape, name, purpose, boiling, and everyday experience. This does not make the kettle “unreal.”
  • “Philosopher”: “love of wisdom,” “direction from lack of clarity toward clarity,” “striving for clarity.” Direction belongs to the example's conceptual content; it does not impose ontological time or objective improvement of the world.

8.5. Film [E]

A character, viewer, projector, screen, and cinema can all be part of the film's own story. They need not automatically be accepted as the external grounds of the depicted film. This is an analogy of self-inclusion, not proof that no material cinema exists in the physical world. No necessary meta-observer “outside the frame” should be added.

8.6. Dream [E]

A dream is described as a dream with distinctions of images and narrative; a character in the dream need not be its separate owner. The example illustrates the model's reach beyond the structure of everyday waking experience. It neither claims that physics is absent from every dream nor proves that waking life is a dream.

8.7. Doubt [E]

Doubt about the model, phenomenon, or experiencing is itself distinguished within experiencing. But this is not a deductive guarantee of the truth of every thesis. A critic may dispute the inference from the occurrence of doubt to the universal “everything.” Self-application grants no immunity from external logical assessment.

8.8. Other universes [E]

“Other universes” is an intelligible conceptual description within experience, including thoughts of worlds with different laws. The model is not confined to the physics of one world. This does not prove the physical existence or nonexistence of other universes.

8.9. Anna and Boris [X]+[R]: an independent example, not from the original draft

Anna, Boris, pain, and reports of pain provide an independent research case, not an essential part of Model of the Phenomenon as originally written. The experiencings and the statements are distinguished; any hypothesis about separately distributed independent perspectives is assessed only where actually introduced.

8.10. Oil painting: distinguishing the picture and its brushstrokes without erasure [AN]

The analogy: within the experience of a painting, both the depicted scene and strokes of oil paint can be distinguished. Looking at the strokes does not erase the faces, landscape, or other distinctions in the image. What matters is the context of the examination—not a separate entity called “the image” hidden behind the paint, nor the elevation of paint into the model's supreme ontological cause. [R] The painting does not prove all five theses; the analogy must not silently install the materialist theory of a physical substrate that the position declines to introduce as its ground.

8.11. “God / hallucination / imagination”: distinguishing truth without a supreme arbiter [AN]

The same experienced content can be described as divine, hallucinatory, or imagined within different conceptual structures. Neither the chosen word nor the coherence of the account creates a second entity “behind experiencing.” At the same time, particular investigations, criteria, and disagreements remain meaningful distinctions within experience. [R] The model does not entail that all three accounts are equally probable, true, or without practical consequences; nor does it prescribe the opposite assessment.

8.12. Water, kettle, neighbor, and dream: no universal script prescribed by the phenomenon [AN]

Within a particular world, water, heating, and the expectation of boiling are distinguished. In a dream, a kettle might turn out to be a neighbor, and water need not behave according to waking physics. Model of the Phenomenon neither sets rules of admissibility for these cases nor judges them by one external standard. It does not claim that a person and kettle are physically interchangeable within ordinary worldly practice. Tests, errors, conclusions, and expectations belong to the relevant conceptual structures.

8.13. “The report was sent yesterday”: analysis without compulsory error [C; R]

An independent test case [C; R], not included in the original work. “The report was sent yesterday” distinguishes a document, sending, communication, recipient, “yesterday,” and a relevant temporal determination. This sentence does not, by itself, posit a temporal axis as an independent nature of the phenomenon. The analysis need not find a mistake: the statement may be an ordinary and perfectly appropriate reference to the past.

9. An atlas of the original work's seven subject areas

All the areas below are fields of application, not scientific theories proven by the five theses. The full scope of (A) is retained: the model is not reducible to the question of “subject versus consciousness.”

9.1. Being [E: group 1]

Topics: the external world; matter; objectivity; reality and illusion; “I” and world; “we”; boundaries; the thing-in-itself; language; nonbeing and nothingness; emptiness; beginning; properties; “one”; self-application; causality, determinism, regularity, change, modality, first concept, fractal, systematic relations; finitude and infinity; time, space, “now,” “here,” and “there.”

Typical error: independently existing first grounds are inferred merely from distinctions between objects or areas of experience.
Question from within the model: “Through which distinctions is this description given, and where is what is distinguished required to be an independent existent?”
Avoid: treating the conceptual character of “thing-in-itself” as a conclusive refutation of Kant; using the account of time to refute physical clocks; inferring the numerical identity of all things from the refusal of an initial ontological division.

9.2. Consciousness [E: group 2]

Topics: observation; perception; consciousness; states; content; location; “I am conscious”; the brain; subject and object; “I” as distinction; other minds; nonduality; owner of experience; solipsism; qualia.

Typical error: an owner, observer, or “inner screen” is introduced as the cause of experiencing itself.
Question from within the model: “Where are consciousness or the ‘I’ distinguished, and what is added when their independence is asserted?”
Avoid: giving the model a global omniscient subject; reducing the quality of pain to the word “concept”; attributing a separate soul to a rival philosophical position without checking its text; demanding an axiom of shared access to experiencings without demonstrating its necessity from the five theses.

9.3. God and religion [E: group 3]

Topics: soul; death; immortality; religion; prayer; miracles; karma; reincarnation; liberation.

Typical error: the sacred, soul, source of the world, eternity, or postmortem transition is introduced as an independently existing condition of the phenomenon itself.
Question from within the model: “What are the conceptual distinctions of life/death, address/response, cause/effect, and obstacle/liberation?”
Avoid: claiming proven immortality, denying biological death, declaring religions proven false, or equating the model with a particular Buddhist school.

9.4. Freedom [E: group 4]

Topics: free will; choice; will; motivation; cause and effect; purpose; action and agent; responsibility.

Typical error: a separate, already constituted subject, a freely causal will, or necessary causal determination is inserted as a first principle.
Question from within the model: “Through which distinctions are action, choice, consequence, responsibility, and their attribution described?”
Avoid: dismissing legal responsibility, practical decisions, or medical causal accounts simply because their vocabulary is conceptual.

9.5. Meaning [E: group 5]

Topics: meaning of life; preference and value; good and evil; justice; conscience; suffering; love; beauty; art.

Typical error: value, norm, moral bearer, aim, or “ought” is turned into a property of the phenomenon.
Question from within the model: “Through which relations among distinctions are meaning, agreement, preference, and suffering experienced?”
Avoid: saying pain is “merely a mistake in thinking,” trivializing suffering, or inferring from the absence of a separate owner that people have no need to help one another.

9.6. Cognition [E: group 6]

Topics: truth and falsehood; delusion; error; proof; logic; knowledge; language; number; science; theory; understanding.

Typical error: a criterion of verification is elevated to a property of the phenomenon, or verification is dismissed altogether by the word “concept.”
Question from within the model: “What distinctions and norms make it possible to say ‘true,’ ‘mistaken,’ ‘verified,’ or ‘measured’?”
Avoid: granting the model immunity from criticism, equating consistency with proven ontological truth, or ignoring the actual robustness of scientific practices.

9.7. The Other [E: group 7]

Topics: development; progress; novelty; culture; society; power; artificial intelligence; learning; history; creativity.

Typical error: collective, agent, author, creator, progress, history, or artificial intelligence is treated as a necessary separate owner or source of experiencing.
Question from within the model: “Which systems of distinctions constitute ‘society,’ ‘progress,’ ‘AI,’ ‘history,’ and ‘the new’?”
Avoid: denying the social consequences of actions; claiming experimentally to have proven either the absence or presence of subjective experience in AI; or reducing a historical fact to arbitrary fantasy.

10. A problem as a concept: examine its premises without mandatory dissolution

[AN; scope] The five theses do not aim necessarily to eliminate philosophical problems, identify false grounds, or purify experience. A particular inquiry may seek to clarify or remove an assumption, in which case its existence, meaning, and role must be shown. A different inquiry may simply examine the substantive structure of distinctions. The model itself changes nothing; a different approach to reading does not establish an active power of the model. See I.12 and II.8.13.

10.1. The model is not obliged to accept a problem exactly as framed in another philosophical theory. A “problem” is also distinguished conceptually. Merely asking a question does not make all its assumptions necessary grounds of the phenomenon.

10.2. If a particular inquiry sets out to examine or dissolve a problem, the following optional check can be used:

  1. State the question exactly, without presupposing the answer.
  2. Identify its distinctions and tacit assumptions.
  3. Examine which assumptions require independent entities, observers, temporal stages, external causes, or modal necessity.
  4. Determine whether any such assumption makes the proposed formulation obligatory. If none is found, report that result accurately.
  5. Only if an unnecessary assumption is established, show why the question is no longer compulsory in precisely that form. Otherwise, do not declare the problem “dissolved” or invent an assumption.
  6. Where the question remains meaningful in a particular world, scientific field, ethical context, or practice, explain how; otherwise, do not assume that it does.

10.3. Invalid move: “The problem is a concept, therefore it does not exist and has been solved.”
Permissible move: “The problem as framed presupposes X. Model of the Phenomenon does not introduce X as a necessary ground. Without X, the necessity of that exact formulation has not been established.”

10.4. “That problem does not arise for us” [AN]. If a starting assumption is not introduced, a particular formulation may not be compulsory within the adopted position. This is not proof that the other question has been solved under its own conditions. Where an inquiry asserts that a problem depends on an additional ground, it must provide an argument for that dependence; otherwise no search for such a ground is mandatory.

10.5. Intellectual boldness need not take the form of opposition to every other philosopher. Historical comparisons are legitimate when the positions are represented accurately and the comparison serves a clear purpose. Such comparisons are not conditions of the model's correctness.

10.6. Testing real understanding rather than repeating approved vocabulary [R; informed by C]

The following is a test of reasoning, not a new thesis or a report of anyone's actual experiencing.

Question: “To whom does the experiencing of pain belong?”

Distinctions in the question: “experiencing of pain,” “to whom,” and “belong.” The word “belong” invokes a relation of ownership, but merely using it does not establish a separate owner as the independent ground of pain.

Assumption worth testing: “For pain to exist, there must be an entity separate from experiencing to which it belongs.” This is a hypothesis about the question's possible structure, not a belief to attribute automatically to everyone who asks it. If “to whom” means identifying the person concerned in a practical situation rather than positing a substantial owner, the stronger assumption is absent.

Analysis: within the model, the experiencing of pain need not be derived from a separate owner. Distinguishing an “I,” saying “I am in pain,” recognizing another person, and communicating pain can all be considered within experience without positing an independent subject as an additional first principle.

What is no longer required: an explanation of why pain would be possible only if a separate substantial owner existed. This rejects the necessity of a particular premise, not the validity of saying “I am in pain.”

What remains: the difference between pain and a report of pain; the difference between “I am in pain” and “she is in pain”; medical and social approaches to recognizing a condition; and the need for care when making claims about another person's experiencing. None becomes indistinguishable merely because it is a “concept.”

What remains open in the relevant practice: how reports are verified in a particular case; how pain differs from a report; how claims about another person's experiencing are justified. A different substantive formulation may not require a substantial owner. These are not holes in the phenomenon's properties that the model must fill, but subjects for further examination of particular experience.

Failure test: “Pain is a concept, so the problem has been dissolved,” or “the question is external and therefore wrong,” fails: neither identifies the relevant assumption nor preserves the distinctions at issue.

10.7. Distinguishing the removal of a premise from refusal to discuss a question [R; informed by C]

If an inquiry seeks to test or remove a particular premise, the exact question, the independently posited ground (if actually present), its role, the distinctions that remain, and the limits of the result must all be shown. A premise absent from the argument must not be attributed to the interlocutor. Ordinary practical questions require no compulsory philosophical dissection. “It is a concept” does not by itself defend the model against substantive criticism.

10.8. “How is the position proved?” is not always an internal requirement [AN]+[R]

Within the adopted position, an assertion can be examined through already distinguished meanings, premises, and additional grounds. The possibility of interpreting an objection in this way does not mean its proponent accepts the first thesis or must regard it as proved. Three tasks must be kept separate: (a) demonstrating an internal mode of reading; (b) criticizing a particular inference in an interlocutor's argument; and (c) externally justifying the starting position. Success in (a) does not automatically establish (c).

10.9. Verification, criteria, and “fewer problems” without a universal law [AN]+[R]

The model makes no independent predictions and sets no universal scale of consistency. Within particular experience, consistency standards, success in testing, prediction, comparison with observation, religious interpretation, and the correction of errors are conceptually distinguished. As one possible consideration [AN], inquiry may ask whether a description introduces further problems, eliminates unnecessary ones, or corresponds to what is experienced. This does not establish the universal law “fewer problems means greater truth.”

11. Modes of inquiry (the operative account is in IV.3)

Exposition of the two works. For Model of the Phenomenon, the five theses [T] are the starting formulations, with definitions and explanations guiding their interpretation. In an exposition of Monophenomenalism, the specialized vocabulary of the model is not automatically mandatory. Terms from other theories—such as “perspective,” “for-me-ness,” metaphysical grounding, physicalism, information theory, temporal dynamics, an objective meta-space, or a specially introduced relation among different people's experiencings—require independent definitions and justification if used. Concrete examples of distinctions are particularly valuable.

Criticism and comparison. Legitimate questions include: What justifies the universal “everything”? Can immediate evidence establish a universal ontology? Does refusal to posit grounds make inquiry unfalsifiable? How should the conceptuality of a representation be distinguished from the independence of what it represents? How does the position differ from idealism, phenomenology, or religious nonduality? Such questions do not automatically establish missing axioms within the model without an argument that the model itself demands them.

Formalization. Predicates, domains, inclusion relations, relations of systematicity, temporal axes, and assumptions about shared accessibility of experiencings among individuals require explicit meanings, domains, and justification for deriving them from the original text. Until their relation to the starting theses is justified, they are independent reconstructions [R] or external formulations [C], not hidden components of the model. A countermodel may show that a proposition does not follow from a chosen set of formal axioms; it does not automatically show that Model of the Phenomenon is incomplete. Formal notation, its interpretation, and the inference itself can also be considered distinctions within experience. A formally satisfiable construction does not establish the truth of its ontological reading.

Editing. Accurate exposition preserves the force of the source wording while distinguishing an adopted proposition, an internal examination, and independent proof. Refusing time as a ground must not be replaced with “we do not know yet,” nor must “the phenomenon has no time” become proof of immortality. A research result is not predetermined and cannot be described as solving another position's problem before its actual formulation has been examined.

Comparison with philosophical traditions. External comparisons with Kant, Wittgenstein, Buddhist authors, Theodore Sider, Eli Hirsch, Amie Thomasson, Dan Zahavi, or David Chalmers require accurate accounts of the claims being compared. No tradition determines the meaning of the five theses in advance. Zahavi, for example, must not be assigned a substantial “I” without evidence; nor may every form of nonduality be equated with the position presented here.

12. Research questions and limits of justification

The following questions require close analysis of sources, further arguments, or careful clarification of terminology. Being open does not indicate a missing property of the phenomenon or license the addition of invented statements to the original works.

12.1. The starting “everything” and the scope of self-inquiry. The first thesis is adopted as a starting commitment. An independent reader may investigate arguments supporting its universal scope. The fact that doubt is expressed conceptually does not deduce the truth of the first thesis. Internal adoption and external proof remain distinct.

12.2. “One” as a concept and “one” as an ontological claim. The starting thesis must not become numerical monism. Nor should it be emptied into a tautology that overlooks its function of refusing independently established further grounds.

12.3. The symmetrical restriction on properties. “The model introduces no properties of the phenomenon” and “the phenomenon has no time” should be read with the clarification that negation does not establish the separate property of timelessness. Neither “the phenomenon is temporal” nor “the phenomenon is timeless by nature” is acceptable as an inferred attribute. The point is that conceptual time is not made an independent criterion for what the phenomenon is. The same applies to space, causality, necessity, beginning, end, and the negations of these terms. This does not demonstrate the impossibility of ontological inquiry under other positions.

12.4. “There is no pure experiencing” and the image of undifferentiated noise. The analogy must not be turned into a historical account of primitive nonconceptual material. Noise illustrates distinguishability, not evidence of an obligatory preconceptual state.

12.5. Recursiveness of concepts. The original draft states that each distinction is systematic and there is no final level. It does not define one mandatory mathematical structure. Trees, sets, fractals, or paradoxical foundations must not be imposed as the essence of a concept.

12.6. “Other minds are concepts” and other experiencings. An idea of someone else's experiencing is considered within experience. It does not follow that other people's pain is false or that one omniscient subject exists. A question from outside the model must not be made an internal requirement without explanation.

12.7. “Death is not the end of the phenomenon.” Death as a concept within a world differs from assigning a temporal ending to the phenomenon itself. Individual immortality, endless temporal duration, and the denial of medical death do not follow.

12.8. Process vocabulary in the draft. The original uses terms such as “arises,” “forms,” “passes into,” “changes,” and “generates.” Interpretations must distinguish ordinary grammar describing experience from an actual process imposed on the phenomenon. The latter should not be reproduced as an unquestionable doctrine.

12.9. “The problem is dissolved” and remaining distinctions. If the earlier formulation ceases to be mandatory, this does not prove that all empirical, normative, or phenomenal difficulties vanish. It should be made explicit which premise is no longer adopted.

12.10. “No ground.” This must not be turned into a prohibition of all arguments or scientific explanations within experience. It refers to refusing an autonomous ground of the phenomenon itself. Arguments, evidence, standards, and causal explanations remain available modes of conceptual description of a world.

12.11. Different senses of “consistency” [C]+[R]+[?]. In (A), consistency is discussed in connection with understanding, logic, proof, scientific tests, and evaluative experiencing. These uses are not automatically equivalent. At least five distinctions may aid assessment: (i) internal noncontradiction of a formulation, (ii) agreement with what is observed in relevant experience, (iii) success of a testable expectation, (iv) justification of an inference, and (v) intelligibility and coherence of meaning. This is a research checklist, neither five new entities nor a list claimed to be from the original work. Any connection among the criteria requires argument. “Consistent” is not proof of the first thesis.

12.12. Pairs of feelings and evaluations [C]+[R]+[?]. In Model of the Phenomenon, love and hatred, pleasure and suffering, and beautiful and ugly function as examples. In each context it matters whether a local definition, illustration, hypothesis, or universal conclusion is intended. Coexisting feelings, the absence of an obvious opposite, and multiple directions of distinction can be investigated. None justifies silently changing the original formulation or declaring the five theses refuted without an argument.

12.13. Ontological adoption and the refusal to introduce [C]+[?]. It remains open precisely how the rule against introducing an independent support differs from adopting the starting proposition, “Everything that exists is a phenomenon. The phenomenon is one.” Discussing that difference does not require attributing a positive or negative nature to the phenomenon. Possible answers need their own arguments and can be explored in particular cases.

12.14. Criticizing the map by its own standards [C]+[R]. Correct vocabulary does not establish understanding. Repeating “distinction,” “not a ground,” and “not a property” without showing what is under examination and what has been clarified risks an empty paraphrase. Only when a premise is claimed to have been removed must the analysis identify which premise it is. Finding none is not a failure of an otherwise accurate examination. Dismissing a critic's question as “external” without analysis is another error. What remains distinguished within experience and what remains open should be explained concretely.

14. An optional outline for extended research

Extended inquiry begins by specifying its subject, task, available sources, and relevant explanations. Section IV.3 governs the choice of approach and assessment of understanding. The five theses are reproduced verbatim when quoted, but need not be repeated every time the subject is mentioned.

For each substantial section, when relevant to the task:

  • The quotation or accurately located source statement [T]/[S]/[E]/[AN].
  • What is being clarified within the model's vocabulary.
  • What openly marked external comparison [X] is relevant and why.
  • Where an entity, owner, time, space, cause, or process might be reified.
  • What does not follow from the material presented.
  • Which questions remain open and whether additional sources, definitions, or arguments are needed for the claim.

The result should answer the question being investigated. Significant checks, independent reconstructions [R], open questions [?], and specific disagreements with sources can be recorded as relevant. K01–K58 are selected by need, not completed for their own sake (IV.3–IV.4).

15. Marking provenance and degree of justification

Analytical prose need only distinguish quoted theses [T], original definitions [S-A]/[S-B], examples [E-A]/[E-B], explanatory notes [AN], independent reconstructions [R], and criticism [C]. Contested claims should also be evaluated by their supporting arguments: justified, tentative, open, or requiring further proof. Provenance and evidential force are independent. Every paragraph need not carry a label.

16. A brief reader's guide (the full rules are in IV.3)

If the originals are accessible, a full reading of Model of the Phenomenon and Monophenomenalism is desirable; independent introductory study through this map is also possible, provided the limits of source verification are acknowledged. Quotations of the five theses preserve their exact published wording. “One,” “phenomenon,” “experiencing,” “distinction,” “concept,” and “experience” do not designate separate entities or stages. Interpretation does not require a hidden agent, independent owner, or causal production of experience; it does not attribute positive or negative properties to the phenomenon. The categorical refusal of time, cause, and subject as independent grounds does not erase the differences of everyday life. The five theses prescribe no goal of finding an error or dissolving every question. Analysis considers concrete content and the mutual determination of distinctions without requiring ordinary language to be replaced by artificial philosophical prose. “It is a concept” does not prove the absence of the object. The original works, their propositions, independent hypotheses, and criticism have different statuses. Full rules appear in IV.3; K01–K58 are used as needed.

17. Navigating Model of the Phenomenon

When consulting (A) directly, semantic sections are more reliable than paragraph numbers in a particular file: “Terms”; “Model of the Phenomenon” and its five theses; thematic implications (being, consciousness, freedom, cognition, and other fields); “Model in One Line”; and examinations through light, noise, film, dreams, doubt, and other universes. Headings and their order should be checked against the edition actually being read on the author's site.

In reviewing an internal exposition, the reader should check that it does not require an independent entity or agent, temporal sequence of production, hidden cause, or other foundation of the phenomenon. This marks the boundaries of the adopted mode of inquiry, not a pre-established proof that every alternative philosophy is false.
