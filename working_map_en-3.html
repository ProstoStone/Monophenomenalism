PART III. DETAILED MAP OF THE POSITION OF MONOPHENOMENALISM

1. Purpose of this part and the position's independence from the full vocabulary of the model

Monophenomenalism is an independent philosophical work and is not reducible to the terminology of Model of the Phenomenon. It sets out a starting position and the reasons for considering it. Adopting monophenomenalism does not automatically require every special definition from Model of the Phenomenon (Sections 7.1 and 7.6).

Section 1.3 formulates the starting proposition as: “Everything that exists is a phenomenon. The phenomenon is one.” “One” does not designate a numerically unique object. Refusing an independently established second ground does not prove that another position is impossible. The starting proposition remains open to independent philosophical criticism.

2. Ontological and methodological aspects: related but distinct

Ontological commitment [S-B]. Everything that exists is considered as one phenomenon. This is a commitment adopted within the position, not a universal theory already proved to every reader. It entails neither a single object or individual, nor a global knowing subject, fundamental substance, or one simultaneous perception of the whole world.

Methodological commitment [S-B]+[AN]. No independently established entity beyond the phenomenon is introduced as a starting explanatory principle. This refusal is substantive within the adopted position, but does not establish that “a second ground has been proved nonexistent.” It is not doubt for doubt's sake: it is a discipline of inquiry that declines to install unestablished supports in advance.

Limit of inference [S-B]. “All assertions available to us are formulated within experiencing” does not entail “everything that exists in reality is identical with experiencing.” The draft of (B) explicitly recognizes this boundary (2.1–2.4). It must be retained even in rigorous internal uses of the five theses.

Clarification of the starting commitment [AN]

Monophenomenalism is not motivated as a gradual economy of premises turned into an ontology. It is a position adopted at the outset: everything is one phenomenon, without positing an independent second ground. External justification of that commitment is another question; the epistemic argument in (B) is not a deductive proof of the first thesis. The clarification sharpens the difference between ontological adoption and a temporary methodological “we shall not introduce it yet,” without claiming that “one” is a separate entity with established properties. Conceptual expressions—including “position,” “adoption,” and “ground”—do not exist outside experience as an operative mechanism.

3. Three premises in (B) and their strictly limited conclusion

(B), 2.1: There is experiencing; doubt about experiencing is itself presented in experiencing. (B), 2.2: Accessible assertions about the world, matter, subject, or external are formulated within experiencing. (B), 2.3: Accessible verification and justification are likewise presented within experience; one cannot establish independence by literally occupying a standpoint entirely outside all experiencing.

The limited conclusion: description and testing take place within experience; caution is warranted about an absolutely external standpoint of verification. What does not follow: unreality of objects, invalidity of science, impossibility of indirect arguments for realism, or the deductive truth of monophenomenalism.

(B) explicitly recognizes indirect arguments, stable observations, and predictions (2.3). The impossibility of a completely external standpoint must not be inflated into the impossibility of all forms of justification.

Language qualification. (B) sometimes refers to “an act of thought,” “verification taking place,” or “the investigator adopting a position.” These are ordinary academic expressions about reasoning, not the addition of an ontological agent, process, or clock to the phenomenon. An exposition must distinguish grammatical usage from ontological commitment.

4. What the position does and does not claim (B, Section 3)

Adopted: everything that exists is considered as one phenomenon; no independent second principle is introduced as a necessary support; particular descriptions require additional arguments. The position does not predetermine all distinctions within the phenomenon or impose a single structure of world, physics, ethics, religion, dream, or waking life.

Not derivable: “only my consciousness exists” (solipsism), “matter does not exist,” “the real world is an illusion,” “all people are identical to one object,” “all experiencings are available to one center,” “personal immortality is proved,” “scientific testing is impossible,” or “the model replaces every other theory.”

A subtlety concerning negation. “Monophenomenalism does not claim X” states a limit of the work and its position. It does not conclude that ¬X is a metaphysical property of the phenomenon. A categorical “there is no time” in (A) should not be rendered as the ontological claim that “the phenomenon is timeless by nature.”

5. How monophenomenalism differs from the model, and how it does not

Common ground: neither work accepts an independent external bearer or an original division between the phenomenon and another fundamental ground. Neither requires a separate subject-owner as its foundation. Both admit examination and employ concepts within the experience under discussion.

Different emphasis: (A) presents the specific vocabulary “sensory experiencing–distinction–concept–experience,” a five-sentence formulation, its recursion, and examples. (B) expresses a more general philosophical commitment, investigates its justificatory limits, and compares it with other theories. Accepting (B) does not automatically entail adopting the definition of “concept” in (A) (B, 7.2–7.6).

The works have different purposes: Model of the Phenomenon offers a specific conceptual vocabulary; Monophenomenalism formulates the philosophical position more broadly. They do not concern different ontological worlds, and neither is a subsequently proved product of the other.

6. Comparing (B) with philosophical traditions (Section 4)

External literature should be used for specific questions and checked against the relevant sources. Another school must not be imposed as the language of internal exposition. The introduction of a new term does not prove originality: (B) itself acknowledges an earlier related use by Fritz Heinemann (3.5). That bibliographical observation is reported from the original work, not offered here as an independent verification.

Limit of philosophical comparison [AN]. Model of the Phenomenon is not obliged to resolve other theories' problems within those theories' premises. Comparison remains possible where the positions and their grounds are explicitly specified. Success at comparison does not determine the model's truth.

7. Particular examples in (B): status and limits (Section 5)

External world. It is not introduced as an independent source of the phenomenon; neither does “the world is illusory” follow. Every proposition about independence is assessed within experience, but the object spoken about is not automatically identical to the word or thought representing it.

Subject. “I,” “mine,” and “other” are conceptual distinctions in (A); within (B), the absence of a fundamental owner does not prove the absence of people. A hypothesis of jointly accessible experiencings for different people requires separate justification and is not a starting axiom of monophenomenalism.

Death. Death is distinguished within the world of living and experience. Refusal to give the phenomenon a temporal boundary does not turn it into the property of being “eternal” or prove personal immortality. Nor can “no postmortem experiencing is possible” be derived without premises.

Causality and cognition. A cause within experience does not become the necessary ground of the phenomenon itself. Scientific causal models of the world are not thereby disproved. “The absence of a cause of the phenomenon” must not become an established absolute characteristic of what exists.

8. Objections and how to examine them (Section 6)

“Is this solipsism?” Not if solipsism means positing a single fundamental personal “I.” But that answer does not prove the existence of every possible other mind. Concrete distinctions should be examined rather than merely replacing one label with another.

“Is this unfalsifiable?” (B) makes no independent experimental predictions; logical, conceptual, and comparative criticism remains possible. A universal ontological thesis lacking a direct experimental falsification test is not entitled to make logical errors.

“Is it a tautology?” The universal “everything” may operate as a definition. The substantive commitment is the refusal to require a second independent ground. This is a philosophical choice, not an automatic refutation of realism.

“Why one?” Within the position, “one” is neither a number nor a substance. External critics may still question the universal ontological commitment; “the question is itself a concept” is insufficient as a formal deduction.

“What does it contribute to science?” Not new physics or an experimental test, but a way of examining the initial conceptual commitments of scientific discourse while retaining scientific practice.

“Is it merely a new name for an ancient tradition?” Only detailed comparison of commitments can answer this. Neither absolute novelty nor automatic identity with idealism, Advaita, or phenomenology is established in advance.

9. Four statuses of implications in Monophenomenalism (Section 7.4)

Section 7.4 distinguishes four statuses of implications. Their exact meaning is fixed by that section of the source, and an additional classification should not silently replace it:

  1. Follows directly from the position, without additional propositions of the model.
  2. Follows within the model, when monophenomenalism and the model’s additional propositions are accepted.
  3. Is compatible with the position but does not follow from it; independent justification is required.
  4. Contradicts the position or requires clarification.

The fourth class combines two distinct cases that may be separated analytically. Adding categories would then be a research reconstruction, not a quotation from (B). The force of each implication depends on its premises.

10. Logical criticism of (B) without imposing an external ontology

Questions to check include: What is adopted as the starting proposition? What provides epistemic motivation? Which definitions are added? Where does an opponent's argument introduce an independent ground? Are “everything,” “phenomenon,” “external,” and “experiencing” used consistently? Is compatibility mistaken for entailment? An external question need not refer to an independently existing object, but substantive objections to the universal scope of the thesis remain possible.

Boundary between internal and external inquiry. Refusing to regard time as an independent nature of the phenomenon does not impose on the model the internal duty to “explain the nature of time.” Failure to answer a question built on a premise not adopted by the position does not itself refute the position. Whether that refusal suffices in a particular investigation may nevertheless be criticized independently. It matters which thesis is being examined and what premises the inquiry employs.

11. Relating (B) to the explanatory notes

  • Monophenomenalism distinguishes the methodological refusal to introduce an independent ground from the starting ontological commitment. The symmetrical rule in the map explains why neither the presence nor the absence of time or cause becomes a property of the phenomenon under the adopted interpretation. This guides reading without replacing the content of (B).
  • In (B), “ground” can refer to an argument or the starting proposition of a philosophical paper. An independently existing ground of the phenomenon would have a different status: an ontological support posited behind distinctions. The meanings must not be conflated without attending to context.
  • (B) permits philosophical comparison and other methods of inquiry. Internal investigation need not adopt external formulations of problems as its own compulsory tasks. The possibility of comparison does not make it a condition of the position.
  • (A) contains categorical formulations concerning the absence of time and beginning. In interpreting them, negation must not become a positive or negative property of a hidden thing. Literal quotations of both works retain their exact wording.

12. Preparing an independent article or investigation

An independent inquiry can move from the five theses to distinctions involving the “I,” kettle, philosopher, light, noise, film, dream, death, and cause. An independently posited ground is examined only where a particular argument actually invokes it. The issue is whether the ground is necessary for that case or has been imported. Rigorous inquiry need not make questions about a separate subject, ownership of experiencing by a perspective, or common accessibility of experiencings among individuals mandatory internal tasks of the model.

Accuracy matters more than reputational agreement or rhetorical victory. The starting position need not be weakened artificially to satisfy an imagined reviewer, but disagreement with a philosophical tradition does not by itself disprove that tradition.

13. Navigating Monophenomenalism

For orientation, use the source's own section headings: starting proposition, motivation for adopting the position, limits of justification, relations to scientific and practical descriptions, comparisons, objections, and the status of implications. A reference such as “Section 7.4” is meaningful where the edition used has that structure; file-specific paragraph indexes are not part of the philosophical content.

2. A central warning: do not split the terminology into separate entities

“Phenomenon,” “experiencing,” “distinction,” “concept,” and “experience” do not designate five parts of a system that must subsequently be connected. (A) explicitly explains that the division is needed by the text. Each term distinguishes what already exists. Changing terms does not describe a metaphysical transformation or a successive appearance of different things.

The exact fourth thesis is “Concepts structure the phenomenon into experience.” In paraphrase, this must not become a causal mechanism. Green is green as distinguished; the word “green” does not color experiencing or separate greenness from an independently existing green substrate.

3. General rules for reading, application, and criticism

The principal task is to understand, investigate, and accurately represent the claims of Model of the Phenomenon and Monophenomenalism, distinguishing original texts, explanations, independent reconstructions, and criticism. Claims are assessed by arguments and the appropriate criteria of inquiry, not by the author's name. Rules for using this guide neither add properties to the model nor give it new purposes.

The approach depends on the task: exposition of Model of the Phenomenon, exposition of monophenomenalism, analysis of a case, editing, criticism, comparison, or formalization. In complex work, the chosen approach is worth making explicit. An internal exposition cannot silently substitute an alternative hypothesis for the model's commitments; external criticism may employ one openly. Ordinary practical questions need no artificial philosophical procedure.

The specialized vocabulary of the five theses in (A) does not automatically apply to every sentence in (B). The latter's starting proposition, the limits of its argument, and the possibility of other research approaches must be preserved.

General principles:

  1. Substantive inquiry into both works should, where possible, consult the author's website and accessible publications (I.1), establish the editions being used, and check the passages relevant to the question. If the site is unavailable, Zenodo, PhilArchive, or supplied copies may be used. If the current edition cannot be identified, the publication actually used should be specified without claiming it is the latest. If the originals cannot be accessed, provisional inquiry within the map's limits is permissible with a clear note that quotations and contexts could not be checked. An inaccessible source must not be represented as read. A question about a particular edition requires consulting that edition. For repeated local tasks, checking the relevant passages suffices.
  2. When quoted, the five theses are reproduced exactly, without introducing a sixth proposition about the “I,” time, subject, systems, causality, perspectives, or necessity.
  3. An accurate exposition does not introduce as an independent first principle an agent of distinction, owner of experiencing, world behind the phenomenon, time, space, cause, mechanism, or property of the phenomenon. The rejection of each must not become an opposite property.
  4. Monophenomenalism is understood as an adopted starting philosophical position, not as a deductive result of “there is experiencing.” Model of the Phenomenon is not the only possible language for that position.
  5. Examination of a question focuses on the distinctions through which its content is determined. No result is fixed in advance: no additional ground may emerge, the question may remain open, and experiencing need not change. The mere presence of words such as “I,” “cause,” “necessary,” “independent,” or “real” does not establish an independent ground; the assertion's meaning matters. If such a ground is actually posited, the inference to it must be identified precisely. Otherwise the premise must not be attributed to the speaker. Ordinary language need not be replaced with artificial constructions.
  6. Examples from Model of the Phenomenon retain their concrete content: green, light, noise, “I,” kettle, philosopher, film, dream, doubt, and other universes. They illustrate inquiry rather than serve as physical proofs or complete theories.
  7. External criticism examines validity, premises, and the limits of particular arguments. Self-application does not prove the first thesis to every opponent. Comparison with other philosophies is permissible but not compulsory; the claims and evaluative criteria under discussion should be precisely specified.
  8. For a consequential contested claim, the status [T], [S-A], [S-B], [E-A], [E-B], [AN], [R], [C], or [?] may be marked explicitly or stated in words. Explanatory notes do not alter the source's literal text.
  9. Completed inquiry answers the task posed. Important differences from sources, independent premises, and open questions are identified as relevant. Original quotations are not rewritten as editorial improvements. Independent development may produce new examples, distinctions, hypotheses, counterexamples, comparisons, and critical arguments. Their provenance is marked [R]/[C]; they do not automatically become statements from the original works or necessary implications of the five theses. An asserted entailment must be supported by definitions, any additional premises, the reasoning, and its limits. A new conclusion may be justified independently; attributing its wording to the original author requires reliable textual or other evidence of provenance, which cannot substitute for proof.
  10. In checking understanding or carrying out philosophical analysis, a useful outline is: exact proposition → relevant distinctions and their mutual determination → any additional ground → what has been clarified and what remains unproved. A claimed dissolution of a problem must specify the limits of the result. Evidential force is determined by the argument, not the vocabulary: “X is a concept” does not by itself prove X false, erase its referent, or resolve the question.
  11. Formal texts, notation, reading, and logical checks are likewise considered within experience. Formal conclusions are assessed relative to accepted premises and do not become properties of the phenomenon without further argument.
  12. A new philosophical examination takes all five theses together. Mutual determination of distinctions does not require a first cause, originating moment, final atom, or mandatory sequence of recursive stages. The order of analysis organizes an exposition, not the phenomenon.
  13. The choice between asserting and denying X is not compulsory where the question about X itself imposes a characteristic not accepted as applying to the phenomenon. Within particular systems of distinctions, X and ¬X retain their own content.
  14. Verification, correctness, error, scientific explanation, and logic also occur within conceptually distinguished experience. They must neither become universal properties of the phenomenon nor be cancelled within the practices to which they belong.
  15. Comparisons with other philosophies, and examples involving God, film, dream, or painting, do not prove the first thesis or make external questions obligatory tasks for the model.

An inquiry may end when its specific task has been met, significant distinctions have been explained, and relevant uncertainties have been identified. This is the completion of a text, not an end to relations among distinctions. A question already clearly addressed by the map need not be reopened without new reasons. The short checks in IV.4 and relevant diagnoses from K01–K58 can be used for self-review. Available assessments are satisfied / violated / not applicable / insufficient information. A separate checklist table is not necessary unless the task demands one.

Insufficient support for newly added elements. A critical question alone does not require a new axiom; ontological relations do not follow merely from a demand to answer a problem posed under different premises; and the mathematical independence of an artificially introduced symbol does not establish the inadequacy of the phenomenon or the original model.

4. A short self-check and supplementary diagnostics K01–K58

Short self-check: (1) theses and quotations are accurate; (2) no external grounds, agents, or properties of the phenomenon—including negative properties—have been attributed to the position; (3) (A) and (B) are not conflated; (4) “distinction,” “concept,” and “is” are not converted into mechanisms; (5) substantive differences within experience remain; (6) no compulsory unmasking or dissolution is imposed; (7) sources, explanatory notes, research reconstructions, and external criticism are distinguished; and (8) the force of an inference matches the task and its stated premises. Every response need not publicly report on all of these items.

K01–K18 appear in II.13; K19–K58 follow. Each inquiry uses only the questions relevant to its task. Inapplicable checks do not indicate an error. An explicitly external assumption is allowed if it is not attributed to the model without argument.

If a text claiming to be an accurate exposition of (A) or (B) contains a substantial distortion, its exact wording and the difference in meaning should be identified. An external critic is free not to accept the position, and a short practical answer need not include a philosophical investigation.

5. Presenting research results

A useful result answers the question posed, accurately references the available original works, indicates the limits of its conclusions, and includes independent reconstructions or open questions where relevant. Quotations are verbatim; source formulations and editorial interpretations are not conflated. Brief tasks do not need an extended report on every diagnostic check.

6. Consulting original works and using the map independently

  • Original works: The primary source is the author's website. Additional Russian and English publications on Zenodo and PhilArchive are listed in I.1. Accurate textual verification requires access to the relevant text and identification of the edition actually used. An edition of unconfirmed currency must not be called the latest. If an original is inaccessible, investigation within the map's limits remains possible with the absence of direct verification clearly stated.
  • Discrepancies between editions: Where a newer edition of an original differs in meaning from a statement in the map, the relevant passages should be compared. An earlier interpretation does not bind a new publication simply because it appears in the guide. Literal wording of each particular edition is nonetheless preserved in quotations.
  • Status of the map: The map adds no independent ontology to the two works. Its statements have different origins and evidential force: quotation, explanatory note, reconstruction, criticism, and open question. The distinction is essential for independent inquiry by both human readers and AI systems.

7. Research questions Q1–Q8: content and limits

Q1 — time and both sides of negation [AN; ?]. Time and its absence are distinguished through concepts in particular experience. Neither “temporal” nor “timeless by nature” becomes a necessary property of the phenomenon. Brief categorical language is acceptable where its sense and limits are made explicit. The most precise forms of exposition in different research settings remain open to discussion. See I.11.1.

Q2 — systematicity and its description [AN; ?]. Any assertion can be considered through the five theses, but systematicity is not identical to a graph, independent branches, or a sequence of stages. Distinctions described as “past” and “future” may mutually determine experience without any causal event of the phenomenon. Further inquiry may clarify criteria for substantively adequate descriptions of systematicity. See I.11.2 and II.7.

Q3 — internal describability and external justification [AN; ?]. Another philosophical position can be considered as conceptually determined within the model, but this does not prove its adherent accepts the first thesis. Justification of the initial commitment for a reader who disagrees remains a subject for independent investigation. See I.11.3.

Q4 — comparison of philosophical premises [AN]. The model is not required to solve the problems of a different theory under that theory's starting assumptions. Explicit comparison of particular premises is possible, but it is not an internal condition for the acceptance or intelligibility of the five theses. See I.11.4.

Q5 — preservation of distinguished content [AN]. Examination need not alter experiencing or resolve a question. Particular experience retains its differences. Strokes of oil paint do not erase the picture, and attention to conceptual distinctions does not deprive the world, pain, or other experiencings of content. See I.11.5 and II.8.10.

Q6 — argument in independent discussion [AN; ?]. Analysis need not be a contest between fully formed ontologies: the exact assertion, its distinctions, and its premises can be examined. Lack of agreement does not automatically refute the model, but the ability to analyze a statement conceptually does not establish the model's truth for the other participant. See I.11.6.

Q7 — standards of consistency and testing [AN; ?]. Consistency, truth, and error are distinguished through concepts of particular experience; the model does not prescribe physical laws, the rules of dreams, or one scale of truth. Particular testing practices remain subjects of inquiry. This does not entail equivalence of incompatible claims. See I.11.7 and II.10.9.

Q8 — limits of thematic examples [AN]. Examples in Model of the Phenomenon are not to be treated as universal laws governing their respective subject areas. When examining categorical expressions, their literal content and degree of justification must remain distinct, without retrospectively rewriting the source. See I.11.8.

Final reminder. This map supports understanding and inquiry, but is not an independent ontological ground, an external law governing the phenomenon, or a set of further theses. Refusing a property does not become a property; “one” does not become an object; “concept” does not devalue what is distinguished; and analysis need not be arranged to discover an error or dissolve a problem in advance.
