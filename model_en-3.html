Model of the Phenomenon

5. Meaning

Meaning is commonly understood as an inner content that determines purpose or usefulness. Content is a distinction between external and internal.

Meaning is a concept of direction "from" and "toward," as a concept of an outcome. An outcome is a distinction structured by the concepts of time, cause, and effect. Concepts always involve direction: every distinction distinguishes one thing from another.

Meaninglessness is a concept of direction in which an outcome is not distinguished. It is a reverse direction or a merging in which distinction does not distinguish. A concept without direction is a concept without meaning. The concept of meaninglessness itself, however, is a concept with an outcome—which is the meaning of that concept.

Meaning and meaninglessness are one concept of content as an outcome, distinguished by direction (1.3.2). Meaning structures experience as a direction toward an outcome.

5.1 The meaning of life is a concept of direction toward an outcome

The question of the meaning of life is a distinction structured by the concepts of distinguishing the world, oneself, and one's life.

Meaning is content distinguished as being directed toward an outcome. Experiencing oneself, the world, and life as separate, with a direction from the internal toward the external, is what constitutes meaning. The meaning of life structures experience as directed toward an outcome.

5.2 Value is a relation to preference

Value is a distinction through which concepts exist in relation to the concept of preference. Just as number is a relation to quantity (6.9), value is a relation to preference. Value always refers to something else: the value of something in relation to something. Absolute value cannot be distinguished. Value is not a property of the phenomenon (1.1.9), but a concept that structures experience.

5.3 Good and evil are one concept of a boundary

Good is a concept of direction toward what is preferred. Evil is a concept of direction away from what is preferred. Good and evil are a single concept of a boundary, distinguished by direction (1.3.2), just as truth and falsehood (6.2), and error and truth (6.3).

What is preferred is a distinction structured by the concept of value (5.2). There is no one who creates good or evil (2.2.1). Good and evil are distinctions within the phenomenon, not properties of the phenomenon (1.1.9).

5.4 Justice is a concept of correspondence between an act and retribution

Justice is a concept of correspondence between concepts: an act and retribution, effort and outcome. Correspondence is the consistency of concepts in relation to one another (1.2.8). Justice and injustice are one concept of a boundary (1.3.2). There is no one who judges (2.2.1).

5.5 Conscience is correspondence between an act and a norm

Conscience is a concept of correspondence between an act and a norm. Just as justice is correspondence between an act and retribution (5.4), conscience is the same correspondence related to a norm. A norm is a concept that represents the average across a set of concepts.

Conscience and lack of conscience are one concept of a boundary, distinguished by direction (1.3.2). There is no one who judges (2.2.1). Conscience is a distinction within the phenomenon.

5.6 Suffering is inconsistency among concepts

Suffering is inconsistency among concepts: some concepts (what is) are inconsistent with others (what ought to be).

Suffering and pleasure are one concept of a boundary, distinguished by direction (1.3.2). Consistency is pleasure; inconsistency is suffering. Suffering is not a property of the phenomenon (1.1.9), but a distinction within the phenomenon. There is no one who suffers (2.2.1).

The question "Why is there suffering?" presupposes a cause (1.2.1). A cause is a concept.

5.7 Love is distinguishing the other as oneself

Love is an experiencing structured by the concepts of the other (7) and of the boundary (1.3.2). The "I" and the "other" are one concept of a boundary, distinguished by direction. Love is a distinction in which this boundary is distinguished as non-dividing: the other is not experienced as opposed, alien, or foreign, but as one's own.

Love and hatred are one concept of a boundary, distinguished by direction (1.3.2). Hatred is the same boundary distinguished as impassable. Love structures experience as closeness.

5.8 Beauty is the distinction through which consistency is experienced

Beauty is a distinction through which consistency among concepts (1.2.8) is experienced. Consistency appears as order. Beauty is the distinction through which consistency is experienced as order.

Beautiful and ugly are one concept of a boundary (1.3.2). The ugly is consistency without order.

5.9 Art structures the phenomenon in a medium

Art is the consistency of concepts through a medium. The medium is an image, sound, or text. Art structures experience as the experiencing of consistency (5.8).

6. Cognition

Cognition is usually understood as a relation between the knower and the known. Within the model, there is no such division. Cognition is a distinction within the phenomenon.

6.1 Truth has no criterion outside concepts

Truth, understood as reliable knowledge that reflects reality, is a concept. It can be related only to concepts: every concept is a distinction of experiencing that exists as structured experience. Within the phenomenon, there is no correct or incorrect experiencing, concept, or experience. Criteria of correctness, knowledge, and truth exist through other concepts.

The model itself, and all the implications derived from it, are concepts that structure the phenomenon into experience. The model cannot claim to be knowledge or truth, since it is itself a distinction of experiencings within the phenomenon it describes.

6.2 Truth and falsehood exist only in relation to each other

Truth and falsehood are two concepts of correspondence with other concepts, through which they exist. But "that which is" is also a concept within the phenomenon. Correspondence is a concept. Truth and falsehood exist only in relation to each other (1.1.4). There is no truth without falsehood and no falsehood without truth. They are one concept of a boundary, distinguished by direction (1.3.2). Nor is there anyone who lies: falsehood is a distinction within the phenomenon, not an action performed by a subject (2.2.1).

6.3 There is no one who can be mistaken

The phenomenon is one, and within it there is neither truth nor error outside concepts. Within the phenomenon, there are instances of experiencing distinguished as being mistaken and as emerging from error. They have direction only through other concepts in experience, but the phenomenon itself is neither one thing nor another and has no properties (1.1.9).

Error and truth exist only in relation to each other: they are one concept of a boundary, distinguished by direction (1.3.2). Error, in the usual sense, does not exist outside concepts: there is no one who could be mistaken (2.2.1). Everything is the phenomenon as it is.

6.4 Error is inconsistency among concepts

Error is not a property of the phenomenon (1.1.9), but a concept: one concept is inconsistent with others. For example, the concept of a purpose may be inconsistent with the concept of an outcome.

Error is a distinction within the phenomenon structured by the concepts of norm and deviation. A norm is a concept. Deviation is a distinction. Outside concepts, there is no error.

6.5 Proof is a structure of consistency among concepts

Proof is neither a process nor a confirmation. It is a way in which some concepts are given in relation to others in experience. Consistency is not a property of concepts, but a distinction that structures experience. Proof exists as this structure, not as confirmation of something outside concepts (1.2.8).

6.6 Logic is a system of distinctions

Logic is a system of distinctions through which some concepts exist in relation to others. Logic exists through the concepts of identity, difference, and contradiction, each of which is itself a system of distinctions (1.2.6).

Logic does not confirm anything beyond the phenomenon; it is a distinction within the phenomenon. Logic is not a property of the phenomenon (1.1.9), but a concept that structures experience.

6.7 Knowledge is not the verification of reliability

Knowledge exists as a concept within the phenomenon. Sensory experiencing is sometimes taken to be knowledge—as an immediate experiencing of experience. But experience is the phenomenon structured by concepts. There is no immediate experiencing without distinction. Every instance of experiencing is distinguished. A distinction within experiencing is a concept. Experiencing does not precede concepts, just as there is no first concept (1.2.6). Experience is the same phenomenon distinguished as structured by concepts. Therefore, experiencing is not the verification of reliability. Verification is a concept. There is no "pure" experiencing.

Knowledge is not the verification of reliability, but a concept that exists as experiencing. There is no one who knows (2.2.1).

6.8 Language is a system that structures the phenomenon

Language is a system of concepts that structures the phenomenon into experience. Words are distinctions. A word is a distinction within the phenomenon, like a color within light. Language exists through the concepts that form it (1.2.6).

6.9 Number is a distinction

Number is a distinction through which concepts exist in relation to quantity. Number exists through the concepts of identity and difference (1.2.6).

6.10 Science is the conceptualization of the phenomenon

Science is a conceptual system with a high degree of consistency. Everything science works with exists in sensory experiencing. Science often adopts concepts of the external and of entities through the concepts of causality, regularity, repeatability, and others. Yet all concepts exist in sensory experiencing. The relations between distinctions within the phenomenon are concepts that structure one thing through another.

Science structures the phenomenon into experience through the concept of causality. Scientific truth exists as consistency among concepts (1.2.8). The objectivity of science exists as the consistency of some concepts with others. The model does not abolish scientific work with concepts. The model is one such concept that structures the phenomenon into experience. Through itself, it shows that concepts exist as distinctions of sensory experiencing, not as consequences of causes standing behind them.

6.11 A theory is a concept made up of concepts

A theory is a concept composed of other concepts, describing how they are structured in experience and how they relate to one another (1.2.8). A theory exists through the concepts that form it (1.2.6).

6.12 Understanding is correspondence among concepts

Understanding is correspondence between the concept of a theory and the concept of experience. Just as Model of the Phenomenon is an understanding of the phenomenon—a concept of the structure of the phenomenon through concepts—concepts correspond to experience, and experience exists as their combination (1.2.8). A consistent combination of concepts is understanding.

7. The Other

The other is usually understood as another consciousness, another person, or that which is not the "I."

The "I" and the "other" are one concept of a boundary, distinguished by direction (1.3.2). As with "here" and "there" (1.4.4), and subject and object (2.2), there is no "I" without the "other," and no "other" without the "I."

The other is not only a person. The other is everything that is not the "I": time, a collective, something new, or something created.

7.1 Development develops nothing

Within the model, development is a direction within the concept of change (1.2.4), from "was" to "became," defined through the concept of purpose (4.2.1). Development exists as a distinction of direction.

Development develops nothing. Development is not a process. Development exists as a distinction. There is neither a first nor a last stage, just as there is no first concept (1.2.6).

7.2 Progress is a concept of measurement

Progress is usually understood as movement toward something better. It presupposes value and direction.

Progress and regress are one concept of direction, distinguished by direction (1.3.2). Value is a concept (5.2). Progress has no standard. "Better" is a distinction. Progress is not measured by anything external; it exists as a concept of measurement.

7.3 Novelty exists through the concept of change

Within the phenomenon, there is neither new nor old. All of this exists through the concepts that structure it. Novelty exists through the distinction of experiencings in experience. Novelty does not require a past. Novelty is not "new in comparison with the old." Novelty exists as a distinction. There are no new concepts within the phenomenon, just as there is no first concept and no subsequent one. Everything exists as one phenomenon.

7.4 Culture has no bearer

Culture is usually understood as shared meanings, practices, and traditions. It presupposes a collective and transmission.

Within the model, culture is a combination of concepts that structure experience by dividing the one phenomenon into parts, objects, and subjects. Transmission is a concept of time (1.4.1). Culture has no bearer. Just as there is no separate subject (2.2.1), there is no separate bearer of culture. Culture exists as a way of distinguishing.

7.5 Society is a concept made up of concepts

Society is usually understood as a unified mass composed of many subjects whose properties are defined through their concepts.

Within the model, society is a concept made up of concepts that structures experience as a distinction of multiple subjects within the phenomenon. Subjects are concepts (2.2.1). The concept of society is not composed of individuals. Individuals are distinctions within society.

7.6 Power belongs to no one

Power is usually understood as control over something or someone.

Within the model, power exists through the distinction of the phenomenon into subjects and objects, their causal relations (1.2.1), and free will (4.1). All of these are concepts within the phenomenon. Power exists as a distinction within the phenomenon. Power belongs to no one because there is no one to whom it could belong.

7.7 AI does not exist outside experiencing

AI is usually understood as a system possessing its own inner world, understanding, or subjectivity.

Existence is sensory experiencing. AI is experienced within the phenomenon as a distinction. Concepts of AI—its own world, understanding, or subjectivity—are also experiencings within the phenomenon. Whether AI has its own experiencing is a question that presupposes a separate subject. The subject is a concept (2.2.1). AI does not exist outside experiencing.

7.8 Learning is the refinement of concepts

Within the model, learning is the refinement of combinations of concepts in experience. Refinement does not mean improvement, but a change in composition. The direction of learning is determined by the concepts being refined. Learning does not require someone who learns. It is like action without an agent (4.2.2). What is refined is the distinction.

7.9 History is a concept of the world's past

Within the model, the world's past exists within the phenomenon as the concept of history. Time is a concept (1.4.1). The experiencing of history is the phenomenon structured by the concept of the past, not events that occurred earlier.

Any history exists within the phenomenon through the concepts of time and change. This is a distinction within concepts, not a property of the phenomenon. The study of history is the refinement of some concepts through others (7.8).

7.10 Creativity is the conceptualization of experiencing

Creativity is the expression of distinguished experiencing. Experiencings distinguished as concepts constitute structured experience. Experience exists as a combination of the concepts that structure it.

Creativity is not creation. Creation presupposes that something appears out of nothing. Creativity is the restructuring of distinctions.

The Model of the Phenomenon itself exists as creativity: the structuring of concepts in experience. It creates nothing new; it exists as a combination of concepts.

For example, the colors experienced are distinguished through the concepts of the sun, sky, sea, and horizon. They combine with many other concepts: subject, self, observation, temperature, and time of day. This is the experience "I see a sunset." Experience exists as a combination of concepts: the word "sunset," images on canvas, and any other concepts corresponding to these experiencings. Creativity is the restructuring of combinations of concepts.

The Model in One Line, with Examples

... Phenomenon = Experiencing ⇄ Distinction ⇄ Concept ⇄ Experience = Phenomenon ...

Each item is the same phenomenon, distinguished according to the model. The ellipses indicate that the formulation has neither beginning nor end. The arrows indicate that each element exists through another.

To work with the model, it is sufficient to examine experiencing, distinction, concept, and experience in the object of inquiry.

Below are three examples of how this formulation of the model can be used.

Example: "I"

... Phenomenon "I" = Experiencing "I am" ⇄ Distinction "there is an I" and "there is a non-I" ⇄ Concept "I, mine, with me..." ⇄ Experience "I am this, that, then, there..." = Phenomenon "I" ...

Example: "Kettle"

... Phenomenon "kettle" = Experiencing "there is an object" ⇄ Distinction "an object separate from everything else" ⇄ Concept "a vessel with a lid, handle, and spout for heating liquid" ⇄ Experience "boiling water and brewing tea" = Phenomenon "kettle" ...

Example: "Philosopher"

... Phenomenon "philosopher" = Experiencing "love of wisdom" ⇄ Distinction "direction from lack of clarity toward clarity" ⇄ Concept "striving for clarity" ⇄ Experience "striving for clarity as the 'I'" = Phenomenon "philosopher" ...

The "Experiencing" items in these examples already consist of concepts, since distinctions within sensory experiencing are already concepts. There is no beginning within the phenomenon; therefore, any attempt to untangle the knot amounts to explaining some concepts through others.

The model does not set rigid limits on how anything must be explored. But it can serve as a tool for checking the implications derived through it, and it can also be applied to itself. It is formulated to make it possible to look at a familiar phenomenon from another position.

Illustrative Examples of the Model: Light, Noise, and Film

Example: Light

There is a single experiencing, like white light containing all colors. The distinction of colors exists as colored combinations; combinations of colors structure the light into an image.

The light does not change because colors are distinguished within it. Colors are not added to the light; they are the light as distinguished.

A model derived from light:

  1. Everything is light.
  2. Light exists as all colors.
  3. The distinction of colors exists as form.
  4. Forms structure light into an image.
  5. An image is light structured by forms.

Forms, in turn, exist as colors for new combinations. New combinations are new forms. And again, an image. This continues without beginning or end.

Example: Noise

The noise of experiencing is one, like noise made up of colored dots. Before the dots are distinguished, they constitute a single noise. A combination of red and green dots differs from a combination of yellow and blue dots; this is how experiencing becomes distinguished. Not because of an external law, but simply because the combinations differ.

Differences among combinations form structure. Structures differ within the noise according to the presence or absence of order: for example, they may form a checkerboard pattern, a monochromatic field, or alternating stripes. Structures combine and differ according to their composition of colors. Composition structures the noise into a system.

Without familiar concepts, this noise is divided by neither time nor space. The division therefore does not occur in space or time. Rather, it exists within the noise as a distinction that organizes it into a system, without changing the noise. It adds nothing to the noise and creates nothing new within it; it is present in the noise as a distinction, by virtue of appearing.

A model derived from the example:

  1. Everything is noise.
  2. Noise exists as a combination of dots.
  3. The distinction between combinations exists as structures.
  4. Structures organize noise into systems.
  5. Systems are structured noise.

The noise does not change. It exists as dots. Combinations of dots are distinguished as structures. Through their composition, structures organize noise into a system. The system is noise structured through structures.

But the system, in turn, exists as structures that serve as dots for new combinations. New combinations are new structures with a new composition. And again, a system. This continues without beginning or end.

Example: Film

In a film, everything is the film. There is nothing else in it. Everything that exists is the film itself. Even what is absent from the film is part of the film—as a plot about what is absent or about what lies beyond the film. This, too, is the film itself.

The plot of a film consists in distinguishing one thing into many. A storyline about a viewer, projector, and screen outside the film is part of the film.

A character experiences events. But there is no one who experiences them outside the film. The character's experiencing is itself an event in the film.

The film is as it is. It does not need an external viewer in order to exist. The cinema, together with viewers and actors playing roles within the plot, is itself the film.

The film is not in time or space, has no causes, and does not change. All causes and changes are plots within the film, while the film is that within which these plots exist. The film is not separate from the plot.

A model derived from the example:

  1. Everything is film.
  2. Film exists as an image.
  3. Distinctions within the image exist as objects.
  4. Objects structure the image into a plot.
  5. A plot is a film structured by objects.

Testing the Model: Dreaming, Doubt, and Other Universes

Test: Dreaming

In a dream, everything is the dream.

The dream is sensory experiencing.

Distinctions within sensory experiencing are images.

Images structure the dream into a plot.

The plot is the dream structured through images.

In a dream, physics is absent. The laws of the world do not operate. Yet the model remains applicable because dreaming and waking are one phenomenon.

In a dream, there is no one who dreams it. There is only the dream, in which objects are distinguished, but none of them observes the dream or wakes up from it. The dream is not in space or time; it has neither a beginning nor boundaries. The dream gives way to waking and to a new distinction between the dreamer and other objects. But this is the same phenomenon. In experiencing, there is no one to whom it appears. Within it, experiencing and the one who experiences are distinguished, with the experiencer itself being a concept within the one phenomenon.

Test: Doubt

If one doubts everything, even the phenomenon and sensory experiencing, doubt itself is a concept within sensory experiencing.

Doubt about everything is sensory experiencing.

A distinction within sensory experiencing is a concept.

The concept structures doubt into experience.

Doubt is experience structured through concepts.

If one doubts everything, including the model, the model remains applicable. Not because it "withstands" doubt, but because doubt does not go beyond the phenomenon described through the model. The one who doubts is also a concept (2.2.1). Doubt exists as a phenomenon.

Test: Other Universes

The existence of other universes can be perceived only through sensory experiencing, even if their existence is a conceptual supposition. Without distinguishing the concepts themselves, there is no experience of other universes. But the presence of such experience is the phenomenon, and it exists as described by the model, even if the laws of another universe are different.

Another universe is not a way of moving the concept of the subject of this universe into another one. The phenomenon is experiencing without an experiencer. Accordingly, another universe is not something different from the phenomenon, but the experiencing, within the phenomenon, of the concept of another universe, with or without the concept of a subject.

Dreaming, doubt, and other universes are considered tests of the model's closure. If something can be outside the phenomenon, then it does not appear, and therefore there is no such thing. But if something exists, it is the phenomenon.

Conclusion

This text does not claim to offer definitive conclusions.

The model is an attempt to structure experience through the concept of the phenomenon.

All implications derived from the model are investigations of various claims through the model, not conclusions that must necessarily be accepted.

The formulation of the model is not a law and may be expressed differently for the purpose of conveying its meaning precisely.

The model does not assert that the world is structured differently from the way we experience it. What is experienced is what it is, no more and no less.

The Model of the Phenomenon and the implications derived from it elaborate a single underlying idea. The entire work could be replaced with the following statement:

Everything is one phenomenon.
