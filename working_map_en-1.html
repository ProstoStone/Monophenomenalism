WORKING MAP: MODEL OF THE PHENOMENON AND MONOPHENOMENALISM

A guide to understanding, investigating, and applying two philosophical works
Author of the original works and their explanatory notes: Roman Dudnikov
ORCID: 0009-0003-2894-7386
https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23651632
r.dudnikoff@gmail.com
Guide, Version 2.0, October 25, 2026
Original works and current publications: https://prostostone.github.io/Monophenomenalism

Abstract

Working Map: Model of the Phenomenon and Monophenomenalism is a general-purpose guide to understanding, investigating, applying, and critically examining two philosophical works by Roman Dudnikov.

The document brings together a conceptual glossary, explanations of central propositions, worked examples, methodological guidance, and criteria for checking the accuracy of interpretations. Particular attention is given to distinguishing the original theses, the author's explanatory notes, research reconstructions, logical implications, and independent criticism.

The map helps identify shifts in meaning, unsupported assumptions, and unwarranted inferences without making the detection of an error or the dissolution of a problem a compulsory outcome. It supports different kinds of inquiry, from initial study and analysis of particular cases to philosophical comparison, formalization, and the independent development of further lines of research.

The guide includes thematic examples, open questions, and 58 diagnostic checks to be used as relevant. It also sets out principles for consulting the original publications, distinguishing editions, and assessing the justification of new conclusions.

The document is intended for independent use by human readers and artificial intelligence systems. It does not replace the original works, establish their truth, or constitute an additional philosophical theory. Its purpose is to support accurate, coherent inquiry that remains open to criticism and further independent investigation.

Keywords: Model of the Phenomenon; monophenomenalism; working map; philosophical methodology; interpretation; conceptual analysis; critical inquiry; philosophy of experience; artificial intelligence.

PART I. FOUNDATIONS FOR READING AND SOURCE MATERIALS

1. The two original works and this map

This guide concerns two philosophical works. For ease of reference, the labels (A), (B), and (C) are used throughout:

  1. (A) — Model of the Phenomenon (Модель явления). The original work containing five foundational theses, definitions, examples, and discussions of their application.
  2. (B) — Monophenomenalism (Монофеноменализм). A separate exposition of the philosophical position, its motivation, limits, and possible approaches to inquiry.
  3. (C) — this Working Map. A guide to reading both works, comprising explanations, a glossary, analyses of particular cases, limits of inference, open questions, and diagnostic criteria. The label (C) denotes the present text, not a particular file format.

Primary access point: https://prostostone.github.io/Monophenomenalism. The author's website hosts the Russian and English texts of (A) and (B), current editions, and related materials. The address identifies the works' publication page rather than a particular file.

Additional publications on Zenodo and PhilArchive:

  • (A), Russian, Модель явления: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23251395; PhilArchive.
  • (A), English, Model of the Phenomenon: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23461875; PhilArchive.
  • (B), Russian, Монофеноменализм: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23375020; PhilArchive.
  • (B), English, Monophenomenalism: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23462332; PhilArchive.

Selecting an edition. When beginning a new inquiry, the appropriate starting point is the author's website, where any edition identified as current can be established. Zenodo and PhilArchive provide additional access points, but their records may refer to particular editions; an archived record does not by itself establish that its text is the latest. If the inquiry concerns a specified edition, that edition should be used. Where no current edition is identified or its status cannot be established, the publication actually consulted should be named without describing it as the latest. Quotations should, wherever possible, identify the language and version or date of the text. Material from different editions should not be combined without notice: substantive differences should be identified, together with their bearing on the conclusion under discussion.

The map can be used on its own for introductory study and limited inquiry. Accurate analysis or verification of a claim made in an original work requires consulting the relevant source. If the website is unavailable, a Zenodo or PhilArchive publication or a supplied copy may be used. If the original cannot be obtained, the limitation should be stated and inquiry confined to the available material; an inaccessible source must not be represented as having been read or verified.

2. Purpose of the map and provenance of claims

The map is intended to support understanding of the starting position and independent inquiry without silently importing premises from elsewhere. This is the purpose of the guide, not a purpose inherent in the Model of the Phenomenon. The five theses have no predetermined aim or necessary outcome. A particular investigation, paper, discussion, critique, or editorial project may have its own aim, but that aim does not become a property of the phenomenon or a purpose of the five theses. The model is a conceptual formulation of what is being considered, not an operative mechanism that changes experiencing.

Careful inquiry benefits from distinguishing a statement's provenance and status. The labels below are research aids; ordinary prose need not repeat them in every sentence.

  • [T] — the five theses of (A), quoted verbatim; their wording is not to be altered when quoted.
  • [S-A] — definitions and direct formulations from (A).
  • [S-B] — the starting proposition and direct formulations from (B).
  • [E-A] — examples, thematic interpretations, and applications of the model in (A).
  • [E-B] — interpretations, comparisons, arguments, and methodological examples in (B).
  • [AN] — an explanatory note originating with the author of the works and recorded in the map. This indicates provenance, not proof of truth, and does not turn a tentative thought into a final axiom.
  • [R] — an editorial or research interpretation, reconstruction, scheme, or proposal; not a direct quotation from the original work.
  • [C] — independent criticism, an external question, or another researcher's argument. Its inclusion does not entail endorsement or establish its truth or falsity.
  • [?] — an unresolved tension or open question.

Part II also uses [S] (a proposition from (A)), [E] (an example or interpretation from (A)), and [X] (an external formulation). These abbreviations do not change the status of the claims concerned.

When the wording of an original source differs from a subsequent explanation, the exact quotation, explanation, and limits of the proposed interpretation should all remain identifiable. A confirmed clarification can refine an earlier editorial interpretation within its proper scope, but does not rewrite the original or remove the need for independent argument. A preliminary thought remains preliminary regardless of who proposed it. The respective languages and purposes of the two works must also be kept distinct.

Suggested reading route: begin with the five theses (I.3), the distinction between the works (I.6), and the general rules of inquiry (IV.3). Consult the glossary (II.5), examples (II.8), explanatory notes (I.10–12), and diagnostic checks (IV.4) as needed. Section IV.3 is the sole complete operative procedure for using the guide. The summary in II.16 abbreviates it; sections I.8, II.7, II.10, and II.14 address particular cases without creating extra obligations for every inquiry. Checks K01–K58 in II.13 and IV.4 are selective rather than a mandatory form to complete in full. Other sections supply explanations, examples, terminology, and open questions. Where a brief or reference formulation seems to conflict with IV.3, the full context and the precise original claim should be examined instead of constructing an additional compulsory protocol.

Different tasks need to be distinguished: exposition of (A), exposition of (B), analysis of a particular case, editing, external criticism, and formalization. Specialized meanings from (A) must not automatically be imposed on every statement in (B). A critic may openly consider an alternative hypothesis, but may not silently attribute it to the author or treat it as an internal refutation of the model.

3. The five unalterable theses [T]

  1. Everything is one phenomenon.
  2. The phenomenon is sensory experiencing.
  3. A distinction within sensory experiencing is a concept.
  4. Concepts structure the phenomenon into experience.
  5. Experience is the phenomenon structured through concepts.

Their wording, sequence, and number are preserved exactly as in the published English version of (A). The theses do not describe five entities, five temporal stages, or separate actions. The self-referential structure of the formulation matters: the fifth thesis returns to the first, and rereading the text is likewise experience distinguished through concepts. This recurrence is not a temporal cycle of the phenomenon itself.

4. The symmetrical rule: positive and negative properties of the phenomenon [AN]

In reading the model, properties are not ascribed to the phenomenon as though it were a separate thing; nor do the negations of such claims establish a property of “being outside time” or “having no cause.” The point is to avoid positing an underlying thing with either positive or negative characteristics. The rule is to be assessed by its content and relation to the five theses, not by the provenance of its explanation.

The following table distinguishes three kinds of formulation. The first two assert unwarranted characteristics of the nature of the phenomenon; the third interprets the starting position without making such an attribution.

Concept Positive reification (inappropriate) Negative reification (equally inappropriate) Interpretation within the position
Time “The phenomenon exists in time.” “The phenomenon is timeless by nature.” “The model does not take a temporal axis to be a necessary independent ground of the phenomenon.”
Space “The phenomenon occupies space.” “The phenomenon possesses spacelessness.” “Space is considered a conceptual distinction within experience, not a characteristic of the phenomenon's nature.”
Cause “A cause produced the phenomenon.” “Objective causelessness is inherent in the phenomenon.” “Causality is not posited as the ground of the phenomenon; causal descriptions retain their place within experience.”
Necessity “The phenomenon is subject to necessity.” “Absolute contingency or freedom from necessity is inherent in the phenomenon.” “There is no basis for imposing a modal category on the phenomenon itself.”
Change “The phenomenon changes like a thing.” “The phenomenon has the property of changelessness.” “Change, motion, and persistence are distinguished within experience.”
Subject “The phenomenon has an owner.” “The phenomenon has the property of being subjectless.” “A separate owner is not required as a preliminary explanatory entity.”
“One” “A single fundamental substance.” “The single substance lacks multiplicity.” “The word ‘one’ is itself a concept; no independently established second ground is introduced.”

Logical precision. Declining to use X as a ground is not the proposition ¬X about an object. The fact that X need not be introduced does not establish that X is impossible. Nor is the position merely a psychological “we do not yet know whether X exists”: within the model as adopted, X is not admitted as an independently given starting ground. The force of this refusal is preserved without ascribing properties to the phenomenon.

Two uses of negation must be distinguished. Section 1.1.10 of (A) states: “Within the phenomenon, there are no boundaries, no external, no time, no beginning and no end.” This wording should be preserved when quoting the source, without treating the negation as a metaphysical property of timelessness. A more explicit interpretive formulation is “time is not introduced as a property or ground of the phenomenon itself.” Even this refusal to introduce time limits the description; it does not discover a negative property of the phenomenon.

Self-application. The table, its cautions, the concept of an “absence of properties,” the activity of criticism, and the model itself are all considered within experience. Prohibiting the attribution of properties must not give rise to a new metaphysical property of “propertylessness.”

5. Accuracy of exposition and common misdirections

Inquiry from within the model. An independent “I,” world, external object, brain, cause, space, moment, or bearer of feeling is not taken as a starting premise. Instead, the distinctions through which these descriptions appear within experience are examined. This does not entail the disappearance of things. Green is distinguished by its greenness; a concept does not color experiencing. “Distinction” does not name an operation performed by a distinguisher.

An external problem is not automatically an internal obligation. Before asking “How are the experiencings of separate subjects combined?”, it is appropriate to examine where the independently existing subjects, ownership of experiencing, and demand for combination have been assumed. The issue may remain open in external inquiry; the model need not be treated as a theory required to fill a gap within premises it has not adopted.

Intellectual integrity. Calling something a “concept” does not resolve a question if it merely renames the issue without identifying the alleged additional assumption. Exposing an assumption must not be conflated with proving that everything under discussion is nonexistent. Criticism of any claim remains possible.

6. Distinguishing the two works

Model of the Phenomenon (A). The five theses describe the one phenomenon in terms of sensory experiencing, distinction, concept, and experience. The definitions encompass sensations, emotions, thoughts, and states under “experiencing”; a concept is a system of distinctions. The examples illustrate applications rather than supplying necessary theorems. (A) stresses that these names distinguish the same phenomenon, not several ontological components.

Monophenomenalism (B). The starting proposition reads: “Everything that exists is a phenomenon. The phenomenon is one.” (Section 1.3.) No separately established second ground beyond the phenomenon is introduced as a starting explanatory principle. (B) also explains that there is experiencing and that the proofs available to us are presented within experience, but this does not deductively entail that everything that exists is identical with experiencing or that independently existing reality has been proved impossible (Sections 2.1–2.4). The initial ontological position is adopted, not established by that epistemic sequence.

Relation between the works. The first thesis of (A) states the shared position; the subsequent theses introduce the model’s specific vocabulary of experiencing, distinction, concept, and experience. (B) states the position more broadly and allows inquiry without adopting the entire terminology of (A) (B, Sections 7.1–7.6). Consequently:

  • The five theses of (A) cannot automatically be deduced from the starting proposition of (B).
  • Neither (B) as a deductive consequence of (A), nor (A) as proof of (B), is justified without argument.
  • The works should not be depicted as two unrelated worldviews describing different domains of existence.
  • Refusal to posit a second ground does not prove that “nothing exists” outside an individual's experience.
  • A quotation from a particular edition of (B) must not be silently rewritten in light of later explanations; the original wording and the proposed interpretation must remain distinct.

Two senses of “ground.” Monophenomenalism uses expressions such as “starting proposition,” “philosophical commitment,” and “grounds and arguments.” These concern logical justification and philosophical exposition. They do not make the phenomenon into a fundamental substance, principle, or cause. Logical premises and supposed ontological grounds must be distinguished by their meaning, not merely by the shared English word ground.

7. Three questions concerning “everything”

Origin of the five theses [AN]. They arose from sustained independent investigation of experiencing, rather than the academic combination of existing theories. This explains something about the text's development but provides no proof of the universal thesis and imposes no obligation to accept it.

Ontological starting proposition [T]/[S-B]. “Everything” belongs to the adopted position. The model is not restricted to one physical world, dream, film, hallucination, or individual subject. It does not posit an external second ground. Yet the universal claim is not established merely because the reader's assertions are formulated within experiencing.

Inquiry within experience [S-A]/[AN]. The model does not declare the experienced world false, or invalidate the experience of the world and science. Clocks, medicine, natural relations, and social relationships retain their significance as conceptually distinguished within experience. None is transferred to the phenomenon as an inherent nature; nor does this limitation establish the opposite property, that “the world is unreal.”

8. Examining a particular case: reference points

See IV.3 for substantive inquiry, II.7 for an optional more detailed procedure, I.4 for positive and negative properties, and IV.4 for criteria relevant to particular claims. Discovering an unnecessary ground, dissolving a problem, or reaching a particular conclusion is not a mandatory outcome. External arguments are assessed against their stated premises. Ordinary practical questions do not require an artificial philosophical dissection.

9. Frequent interpretive mistakes

  • From unity to a container. Treating “one” as a domain containing separate phenomenal objects and subsequently trying to reunite what was assumed separate. The appropriate starting point does not posit independent ontological grounds in the first place.
  • From a term to an agent. Claiming that a concept “extracts” color, experiencing “creates” experience, or a subject “performs” a distinction. Green is distinguished by its greenness, without an additional mechanism that distinguishes it.
  • From a negation to a property. Reading “the phenomenon is not in time” as the discovered property of “timelessness.” Time is not attributed to the phenomenon as either a positive or a negative ontological characteristic.
  • From an external question to a compulsory task. Declaring the relation between different people's experiencings, or their supposed joint accessibility, to be a missing axiom without showing why the five theses demand such an account. The question's premises should be examined first.
  • From a formal scheme to an ontological conclusion. Varying an arbitrarily introduced predicate can establish the independence of formulas within a chosen axiomatization, but not the need for an additional ground of the phenomenon itself.
  • From conceptuality to unreality. Declaring the “I,” things, death, pain, or scientific norms to be nothing merely because they are considered concepts. A distinction does not abolish what is distinguished.
  • From dialogue to mandatory superiority. Either weakening the starting position to meet external expectations or declaring it proved superior to all other philosophies. Bold inquiry and independent criticism require neither kind of rhetorical victory.

10. Explanations of the central propositions [AN]

These explanations support more precise reading of potentially ambiguous expressions in Model of the Phenomenon and Monophenomenalism. They do not change quotations or add a sixth thesis. [AN] denotes the origin of an explanation, while [R] denotes independent editorial interpretation. The soundness of each claim depends on its content and supporting arguments.

10.1. What “is” means [AN]. In the five theses, the English copula is (corresponding to the Russian есть) is itself a conceptual distinction, as are the other terms in the five theses. In this context it does not attach a property to an independently existing object or establish a relation between two separate entities. “The phenomenon is sensory experiencing” does not mean “there is a phenomenon behind experiencing, and experiencing is its quality or means of appearing.” As one explanatory formulation puts it: “We call the phenomenon ‘experiencings’; we have divided it in words.” The phrasing makes distinctions in experience through language; it does not license an additional nature of the phenomenon behind the words. [R] This should not be confused with a mathematical identity sign, a universal ontological proof, or the claim that no content is distinguishable at all. The grammatical function of is in English may differ from that of есть in Russian; the philosophical point concerns the interpretation of the thesis, not a universal rule of English grammar.

10.2. “One” and the starting position [AN]. Monophenomenalism is not a program in which an investigator gradually eliminates extraneous grounds. It is adopted from the outset as a position that introduces no independent ground beside the one phenomenon. “One” is itself a concept, not a name for a single fundamental object. [R] Its difference from mere methodological economy concerns the starting commitment, not the discovery of positive or negative properties of a supposed “one.” This commitment cannot be deduced from explanatory economy alone.

10.3. Inescapability within the adopted model [AN]. An explanation of the starting position states that a denial of the first thesis, when considered as appearing within the phenomenon, is itself describable through the model: “To reject it, one would have to adopt a position outside the phenomenon.” Analogies include a fish in water and using mathematical formulas to deny mathematics. [R; logical limit] Applying a description to an objection does not prove the initial universal thesis to someone who has not adopted it. A false or inconsistent statement can also be written in the notation of a formal system; that fact does not make it true. Objections cannot be declared groundless merely because they are expressed conceptually. The internal use of the model, refutation of a particular implication, and external justification of the starting commitment are distinct matters.

10.4. Invariance, absence of cause, time, and process [AN]. Explanatory formulations include: “The phenomenon and experiencings have no cause, but they are structured in experience as though there were a cause,” and “the phenomenon is unchanging.” Such negations must not become negative attributes of a substance or a description of a separate, unchanging substrate. [R] Causal sequences, emergence, change, time, and space may be contents of experience; none is assumed as a ground of the phenomenon itself. Statements such as “one concept changes another” are read within descriptions of experience, not as an independently operating conceptual machine or a required temporal dynamics of the phenomenon.

10.5. The model prescribes no laws or external standards for the contents of experience [AN]. It does not determine which world, physical law, causal expectation, test result, feeling, or event “must” occur. Water may boil or fail to boil, and in a dream a kettle might turn out to be one's neighbor. The model does not prescribe the range of possible contents; it permits the examination of experience already distinguished. Correctness, error, verification, and scientific criteria are likewise conceptual distinctions within the relevant experience. The model must not become an external standard of a “correct” phenomenon. This does not exclude using the model to examine the consistency of an inference under explicitly stated premises.

10.6. Consistency as a possible guide to inquiry [AN]. An analysis may consider whether a description generates further problems or clarifies the ones posed, how it accords with what is experienced, and how much it explains. This is neither a privileged law of the phenomenon nor a universal criterion of truth. [R] Fewer questions do not ensure factual accuracy: a brief false account may seem unproblematic, while a detailed accurate one raises new questions. These standards themselves are considered within experience; they do not become a higher law of the model.

10.7. Thematic examples remain examples [AN]. Kettles, love and hatred, pleasure and suffering, philosophical problems, dreams, and films illustrate possible modes of consideration. They must not be rewritten as universal theorems supposedly deduced from the five theses. An example may contain a strongly worded statement from (A). Whether that statement exhausts the matter calls for argument, not a silent alteration of the source wording.

10.8. Verbal division and experienced distinctions [AN]. “Phenomenon” and “experiencing” are distinct words in the exposition; this verbal difference does not establish an independent ontological divide. “If the words are removed, the division disappears” does not describe a causal operation in objective time or the disappearance of green, pain, noise, and other experienced differences. [R] The absence of independently existing parts does not by itself establish the absence of immediately distinguishable qualitative content.

10.9. “That problem does not arise for us” [AN]. The model and monophenomenalism do not claim to solve every traditional philosophical problem as traditionally posed; nor must they contest every competing position. Their starting commitment may dispense with the premise on which a problem depends. An independent analysis must identify the premises of the specific formulation and explain which of them are not adopted, rather than declare the formulation false for everyone without argument.

10.10. No higher-level ground supplied by the map [AN]+[R]. The model does not predetermine the contents of a world, create an external standard for correct concepts, or describe an entity called “the phenomenon” behind conceptual distinctions. This map too is a conceptual instrument within what it describes; it does not observe the world “from outside.” Expressions such as “do not introduce” and “do not ascribe” govern the interpretation under consideration; they are not a list of properties of a hidden entity.

11. Questions Q1–Q8: explanations and open boundaries of inquiry [AN]

This section examines explanatory responses to eight research questions. Some remain reflections rather than final answers. [AN] identifies the provenance of a formulation, [R] an independent interpretation, and [?] a question that remains open. None adds a sixth thesis or requires that all philosophical problems be settled.

11.1. Q1: assertion and negation rely on the same distinction [AN]

Time, beginning, end, absence, and change are distinguished through concepts within particular experience—for example, a world, an “I,” or finitude. The expressions “the phenomenon has time” and “the phenomenon has no time” may share the assumption that time is a characteristic to be attributed to the phenomenon. The analogy is that assertion and negation are two sides of a coin whose existence has already been assumed. If the coin is not introduced, neither side needs to be affirmed or denied.

[R] Suggested formulation: “Time and beginning are distinguished in particular experience; the model does not make them necessary grounds of the phenomenon itself, nor does it attribute the opposite property of timelessness.” If a shorter formulation such as “there is no time” is quoted, its refusal to impose the question should be explained nearby; it is not a description of a negative nature. Expressions such as “beyond the concept” do not establish an actual spatial region outside concepts.

11.2. Q2: examining any assertion through the five theses [AN]

An assertion can be examined through the five theses by asking which distinctions give it determinacy, where something distinguished is treated as a primary ground, and how its conceptual determinacy depends recursively on other distinctions. This is not a search for the temporal birth of a representation, a first cause, or a final element. Phrases such as “where it arises” or “the next level” may locate a relation in conceptual determination without implying a moment or a spatial organization of the phenomenon.

[R] One inspectable outline: exact assertion → words and experiencings distinguished within it → their mutually determining conceptual distinctions → any additional independently posited ground, if one is found → what remains within experience. This is a way of recording an analysis, not a sequence of existence or proof that systematicity must take one particular form. Neither a graph nor a logical formula replaces the definition of a distinction as itself a system of distinctions.

11.3. Q3: opposing positions do not constitute a second world beside the phenomenon [AN]

Within the adopted position, even opposed philosophical views can be considered through the one phenomenon: the independent ground they posit is itself distinguished conceptually in a particular experience, rather than being a neighboring object outside the phenomenon. Agreement and denial are likewise distinguished within the position. [R] The fact that another position can be described in the model's vocabulary does not mean its proponent accepts the first thesis. An internal examination must be distinguished from proving a position to someone who rejects it.

[R; strict limit] “Every position already starts from the one phenomenon” applies to how positions are interpreted within the model, not to what every philosopher knowingly endorses. Being describable in the model's language (a) does not logically compel an interlocutor to accept its ontological commitment (b). External justification remains an open question [?], but it does not automatically demand an additional entity.

11.4. Q4: questions from other philosophies are not compulsory tests [AN]

The model is not required to solve problems framed by assumptions particular to idealism, phenomenology, physicalism, or another interlocutor. Comparative inquiry may examine the actual premises of each formulation [R] without making success on an imported problem the test of the five theses. Comparison is a distinct research activity, not an internal condition of their truth.

11.5. Q5: distinction does not erase the world—the painting example [AN]

Recognizing that an oil painting consists of strokes of paint does not erase what the painting depicts. It changes how the depicted scene and its context are distinguished. Similarly, considering a world, person, pain, or question through conceptual distinctions does not remove the corresponding experiencing or require it to become an undifferentiated mass. Particular cases still need particular examination: calling something a “concept” does not specify all of its content.

[R] “The model changes experiencing” should not be read as though a concept were acting on an object. A less misleading description is that the same experience can be distinguished differently when considered through the model. This is not an empirical law governing changes in other people's perception.

11.6. Q6: examining an assertion rather than competing over first principles [AN]

Rather than making philosophical inquiry a compulsory contest between complete ontologies, a particular assertion can be examined for what it presupposes, which distinctions determine its content, where an independent ground is asserted, and whether that ground is needed for the case at hand. This guarantees neither discovery of a first cause nor final truth nor universal agreement. “There is no assertion outside the phenomenon” is a statement within monophenomenalism, not a premise already proved to every opponent.

11.7. Q7: consistency, verification, and truth are also distinguished within experience [AN]

An illustrative experiencing may be distinguished as divine, hallucinatory, imaginary, and so forth. Within particular conceptual accounts, coherent arguments may permit or exclude God. This does not thereby establish an independently proved essence of the experiencing. Verification, correspondence, truth, correctness, and consistency are themselves determined through concepts within relevant experience. The model is not a supreme law of consistency and does not dictate the rules governing a world, a dream, physics, or their tests.

[R; qualification] The conceptual character of verification does not imply that incompatible claims are equally supported within a given practice, that all predictions succeed, or that scientific and medical testing can be disregarded. Such standards may have substantial force in particular experience without becoming a law above the phenomenon. The figurative remark that “it is all noise / a heap of rubbish in which structures are distinguished” points to the absence of an independently existing meta-entity behind structures; it is not a dismissal of actual experiencing or scientific practice.

[?] Open to further examination: what exactly “true,” “consistent,” and “dissolving a problem” mean in particular cases if no external universal standard is introduced. The open question must not be filled by positing properties of the phenomenon.

11.8. Q8: examples and the limits of literal formulations [AN]

The thematic cases in Model of the Phenomenon are examples of inquiry, not exhaustive laws of love, suffering, beauty, knowledge, or all possible worlds. [R] If an expression in (A) sounds universal, its precise wording should be distinguished from a claim about its evidential force. Its meaning and scope may be investigated without altering the original text.

Conclusion to Section 11. “One,” “is,” “within,” “distinction,” “verification,” and even “ground” are themselves available for the same examination. The five theses and the present map are conceptual distinctions within experience; they are not an external law, a higher-level instrument, or a self-proving truth.

12. No inherent goal or required outcome [AN]

12.1. Examination, not intervention [AN]. A formulation of the approach is: “We simply look; we do not change anything.” Experience may be distinguished differently in examination through the model, but this does not posit an active model, a subject, a mechanism of intervention, or a causal process that remakes experiencing. The five theses prescribe neither a loss nor an addition of content. [R] “After examining it” is acceptable ordinary speech about a particular experience; it does not describe temporal stages in the phenomenon itself.

12.2. The five theses have no purpose of their own [AN]. The model is considered a reflection of the phenomenon as distinguished in experience; no inherent purpose is attributed to either the phenomenon or the model. An inquiry might concern instrumental applications, another the philosophical position, and a third the model as a mirror. A goal belongs to a particular investigation, not to the five theses. [R] Procedures, checks, and assessments of success organize research; they are neither laws nor necessary outcomes of the model.

12.3. Neither a tree, a ladder, nor a succession [AN]. Recursive relations among distinctions are not a collection of isolated branches or independent atoms. An analogy with atoms in a universe concerns the impossibility of separating a group of mutually determining distinctions from its context; it is not an assertion of physical atomism. Any distinction considered is determined conceptually through others. Ordering such relations as successive “steps” is an arrangement of discourse, not a discovered structure of the phenomenon. Without a previously adopted temporal axis, distinctions labeled “past” and “future” may jointly determine experience. This does not make them causal events in chronological succession. [R] Systematicity specifies neither a unique mathematical graph, a number of links, nor a necessary spatial arrangement.

12.4. Categorical negations retain their force [AN]. “There is no time,” “there is no beginning,” and “there is no subject” must not be weakened to “we are not yet sure.” They decline to accept the corresponding concepts as independent grounds of the phenomenon, rather than attributing negative properties to a hidden thing. The coin analogy (I.11.1) shows why rejecting the initial dichotomy does not require selecting either “has X” or “lacks X.” The strength of these formulations can be preserved through clarification of their scope. Uncertainty about an argument should not silently replace the position with agnosticism, nor should negation describe a new nature of the phenomenon.

12.5. No compulsory unmasking [R; informed by AN]. An inquiry may clarify the structure of a statement without discovering a hidden independent ground, dissolving a problem, or deriving a new conclusion. When a particular premise is the express object of analysis, the result should be shown. Otherwise, failure to “dissolve” a problem does not make the inquiry unsuccessful. The example in II.8.13 illustrates a legitimate result in which no ontological violation is found.
